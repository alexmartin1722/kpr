# Putumayo River

The Putumayo River or Içá River (, ) is one of the tributaries of the Amazon River, southwest of and parallel to the Japurá River.

== Etymology ==

The Quechua name for the river is 'putu mayu', and the name 'Putumayo' comes from Quechua and historically referred to as Putumayu [cite: 1].
The Putumayo River is called Río Içá in Spanish, and the name 'Içá' comes from Quechua words meaning 'fruit-bearing tree vessel river' [cite: 2].
The word 'Içá' originates from the ancient Tupi term 'ysá', which refers to a female peccary [cite: 11].
The river is known as Valsayacu or Walsayaku in Inga-Kichwa.

The Putumayo River forms part of Colombia's border with Ecuador, as well as most of the border with Peru.
Known as the Putumayo in the former three nations, it is called the Içá when it crosses into Brazil.
The Putumayo originates in the Andes Mountains east of the city of Pasto, Colombia.
It empties into the Solimões (upper Amazon) near the municipality of Santo Antônio do Içá, Brazil.
Major tributaries include the Guamués River, San Miguel, Güeppí, Cumpuya, Algodón, Igara-Paraná, Yaguas, Cotuhé, and Paraná de Jacurapá rivers.
The river flows through the Solimões-Japurá moist forests ecoregion.

== Source and Upper Course ==

The river is called Guamués at its source, which is located in the Nudo de los Pastos region within the municipality of San Francisco and reportedly within the municipality of Sibundoy [cite: 5].
The Putumayo River originates in the Cordillera Oriental 7 km north of San Francisco, a town approximately 70 km east of the city of San Juan de Pasto.
The upper course of the river lies in the Putumayo Department and initially flows in a predominantly southwest direction through the small town of San Francisco.
After approximately 30 km, the Río Putumayo changes direction towards the east-southeast and cuts through the Cordillera Oriental mountain range for a length of 35 km.
At a location 10 km west of the city of Villagarzón, the river reaches the Amazon Basin at the foot of the mountains and soon leaves the Andes mountain range.
The Putumayo reaches the Colombian town of Puerto Asis, and within 120 km of its source, the river has a flow rate of 470 cubic meters per second and drains a basin area of 2900 square kilometers.

The Putumayo River originates as the Guamués River from Lake Laguna de La Cocha, which is located high in the Andes mountains in the southern part of the Central Cordillera of the Colombian Andes near the city of San Juan de Pasto [cite: 8][cite: 9].
The source is located in the Cocha lagoon, and the river rises southeast of Lake Laguna de La Cocha [cite: 9].
The river flows from the northwest and eastward across a plateau covered in dense forests [cite: 8].
The river was named Guamués at its source [cite: 16].
Sibundoy is 50 km east of the city of Pasto [cite: 5].
The river is navigable shortly after its source, and at this stage, the Putumayo carries as much water as the Rhône.

== Hydrology and Physical Characteristics ==

The left bank corresponds to the Colombian side and is higher than the right bank.
The highest population density in this section is located on the left bank due to its elevation, where large areas covered with pastures for cattle ranching are visible.
The Putumayo River basin is narrow and elongated, lying between the Caquetá (or Japurá) River basin to the north and the Napo River basin and the Amazon-Solimões River basin to the south.
The narrowness of the basin forces most tributaries to follow a path parallel to the main river.
The river empties on the left bank with a width of 700 meters at an altitude of 55 meters.
The average current speed varies between 0.5 and 1.5 m/s, while the upper part of the river reaches a speed of 1.5 m/s during average flow periods with an average flow of 500 cubic meters per second.
During low water periods, the speed in the upper part of the river reaches only 0.60 m/s and the flow is 250 cubic meters per second.
The depth of the river varies between 4 and 9 meters.
At the height of the Colombian city of Tarapacá, the water flow velocity is approximately 0.9 m/s, which corresponds to a flow rate of 7000 cubic meters per second.

The Putumayo River has significant water level variations in the upper part of its basin, where differences exceed 2 meters within just 48 hours.
Water levels become more stable in the middle and lower parts of the river basin, with a maximum fluctuation of 0.5 meters in 48 hours in Tarapacá.
Large winter floods cause erosion that leads to major bank slides carrying loose material, clay, and large trees.
These materials form palisades that pose a serious danger to navigation.
Sandbars form along the river and create islands.
The river flows across a plain.

During the Amazon rubber boom of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the land around the Putumayo became a major rubber-producing region, where Julio César Arana's Peruvian Amazon Company maintained a production network centered on the nearby city of Iquitos.
The river serves as a transport route primarily for the rubber industry.
Rubber collected from forests along its course is transported from these ports.
Residents on both banks of the Putumayo River primarily engage in rubber trading.
Leather production is carried out along the riverbank.
Produced leather and balata are transported by boat.
This production network mainly relied on the labor of indigenous Indians, who suffered from widespread human rights abuses.
These abuses were first publicized in 1909 within the British press by the American engineer Walter Hardenburg, who had been briefly imprisoned by Arana's private police force in 1907 while visiting the region; Hardenburg later published his book The Putumayo: The Devil's Paradise in 1913.

In response to Hardenburg's exposé, the British government sent the consul Roger Casement (who had previously publicized Belgian atrocities in the rubber business of the Congo Free State) to investigate the matter; between 1910 and 1911, Casement subsequently wrote a series of condemnatory reports criticizing the atrocities of the PAC, for which he received a knighthood.

Casement's reports later formed much of the basis for the 1987 book Shamanism, Colonialism, and the Wild Man by the anthropologist Michael Taussig, which analyzed how the acts of terror committed by British capitalists along the Putumayo River in Colombia had created a distinct "space of death."

Today, the river is a major transport route.
Almost the entire length of the river is navigated by boats.

Cattle farming, along with the rubber trade, is also a major industry on the banks of the Içá.
Rubber and balatá (a substance very much like gutta-percha, to the point where it is often called gutta-balatá) from the Içá area are shipped to Manaus, Brazil.

On March 1, 2008, Raúl Reyes and 14 of his fellow Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia guerrilla companions were killed while on the Ecuadorian side of the border by Colombian military forces.

In November 2019, scientists from the Field Museum worked with partners from Colombia and Peru to perform a three-week "rapid inventory" of almost 7 million acres around the Putumayo, one of the few Amazonian rivers that remains undammed, documenting 1,706 species.
The goal of these fast surveys of remote areas is to bring together local stakeholders to collaboratively protect wilderness.
