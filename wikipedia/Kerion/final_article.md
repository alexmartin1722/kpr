# Kerion

Kerion or kerion celsi is an acute inflammatory process which is the result of the host's response to a fungal ringworm infection of the hair follicles of the scalp (occasionally the beard) that can be accompanied by secondary bacterial infection(s). It usually appears as raised, spongy lesions, and typically occurs in children. This honeycomb is a painful inflammatory reaction with deep suppurative lesions on the scalp. Follicles may be seen discharging pus. There may be sinus formation and rarely mycetoma-like grains are produced. It is usually caused by dermatophytes (fungal infections of the skin affecting humans and animals) such as Trichophyton verrucosum, T. mentagrophytes, and Microsporum canis. Treatment with oral griseofulvin common.

== Etymology ==

The term Kerion Celsi is named after Aulus Cornelius Celsus, and the name honors him because he is believed to have been the first to describe this lesion.
In ancient Rome, the word 'kérion' designated a honeycomb cell.

== Pathophysiology ==

Kerion is a button-like inflammation of the skin that represents the maximum form of dermatophytotic symptoms.
A kerion is observed only in infections with dermatophytes not adapted to the host, such as a zoophilic pathogen in humans.
The condition is also known as inflammatory and suppurative ringworm.
Kerion Celsi is the most severe form of dermatophytosis.
Human bodies mount a severe inflammatory and granulomatous response to these dermatophyte infections.

== Etiology ==

Tinea capitis is a fungal disease located on the scalp, and these infections most often occur as tinea capitis.
Celso's kerion is generally caused by Microsporum, with Microsporum canis and Microsporum gypseum serving as primary causes of Celso's kerion.
The responsible fungi are essentially anthropophilic dermatophytes of animal origin.
Trichophyton mentagrophytes is found in horses and mice, while Trichophyton ochraceum or T. verrucosum is found in cattle.
Geophilic fungi have also been identified.
The responsible pathogens are primarily Trichophyton tonsurans, Trichophyton verrucosum, and Trichophyton mentagrophytes.
Celsus affects the head.
This fungus is the same one that causes athlete's foot (tinea pedis).
The condition is a zoonotic disease.
The most common cause is a mold fungus.
Trichophyton verrucosum, T. mentagrophytes, and Microsporum canis are types of mold fungus that cause this condition.

Fungi other than dermatophytes can cause kerion.
A hospital in Guangzhou, China reported a case where the fungus Aspergillus protuberus caused kerion.
The fungi that cause kerion are mostly zoophilic, while some are anthropophilic or geophilic.
Trichophyton rubrum is not a common cause of kerion.

== Transmission ==

The disease spreads through contact with animals carrying the fungus, including cats, dogs, horses, and calves.
Ringworm can spread to other parts of the same person's body and to anyone who comes into contact with the infected person.
Family members, classmates, and friends are at risk of contracting ringworm through contact.

There may be loss of hair as hair will come out easily.
Sometimes, there is growth of organisms.
Lymph and fever symptoms may be present.
This condition can be mistaken for a case of impetigo.

== Initial presentation and lesion morphology ==

The condition begins with reddish spots on the scalp, which merge together to form an inflamed disc that rises above the surface of the head.
More than one disc may appear.
Tinea capitis usually begins as a non-inflammatory condition, and the inflammation occurs subsequently.
The kerion appears as a rounded, thick, firm plaque that is scattered with follicular orifices.
Nodules and edema are present.
Red spots appear on the head.
The condition presents with patch-like lesions.
Kerion initially presents as suppurative folliculitis.
Clusters of small pustules form nodules or masses.
The boundaries of the affected area are clear.

== Inflammatory signs and the sponge sign ==

Papules and pustules are generated in the area, and these papules and pustules dry up leaving a crust.
During this period, there is a sign known as the 'sponge sign', which involves observing pus coming out of each hair follicle pore when lateral pressure is applied to the area.
Pus and blood emerge from the follicular orifices, and small pus points on the surface correspond to hair follicle openings.
Squeezing the mass causes pus to overflow through multiple pores, and pus can drain from the fistula tract.
The inflammatory response is significant [cite: 8], and the inflammatory reaction includes redness and heat.
Celsus is accompanied by rashes and hair loss.
The lesions contain yellow pus at the hairline [cite: 4,11].

== Hair loss and follicular damage ==

Hair loss is often the first sign noticed, and the most common symptom is easy hair loss.
Hair loss can leave a circular bald spot with a diameter of 15 cm, while the diameter of the disc is several centimeters.
Mild fluctuation may occur, and hair becomes sparse.
Hair follicles can rupture.

== Systemic and regional symptoms ==

Nearby lymph nodes swell.
Infectious local or general signs may accompany the condition.
Satellite lymphadenopathy may occur.
Muscle aches may occur.
General deterioration of health may occur.
These patients feel generally unwell.

== Distribution and atypical locations ==

In adult men, the condition is mainly found on the beard area.
The condition can sometimes also appear on hairless skin.

== Scarring and long-term outcomes ==

A scar remains in place after the condition resolves.
The healing of a kerion usually results in the formation of hairless scars, a condition known as scarring alopecia.
The process heals in a fibrous manner and leaves areas of permanent hair loss.
The infection is destructive and leaves a residual area of scarring alopecia.
The condition can leave scars, and scarring alopecia may occur.
Scabs may form as a result.
Untreated cases for a long time may lead to permanent hair loss.

== Healing and hair regrowth ==

Hair will regrow after treatment, with one month after the treatment showing hair present as fine downy hair (vellus hair).
Hair regrows six months to a year after treatment, and in most cases, hair regrowth is complete, though the condition can result in thinner hair.
Severe inflammation occurs at the hair follicle level, and widespread eczematous rashes may occur.

== Clinical course ==

Healing occurs spontaneously within a few weeks even without treatment.
The fungus takes an average of 20 to 25 days to reach maturity after contamination, and hair loss begins when the fungus reaches maturity.

== Epidemiology ==

Kerion is especially common in preschool children.

The basis for the diagnosis of kerion is clinical finding, positive microscopic examinations (such as positive KOH preparation, Lactophenol cotton blue wet mount, Chicago sky blue stained (CSB) slide, Calcofluor white stained slide, Periodic acid–Schiff stained slide, and Gomori's methenamine silver stained slide), mycological culture and modern molecular tests (such as PCR-reverse line blot test, real-time PCR test, multiplex PCR test, PCR-ELISA test, and MALDI-TOF test) of clinical specimens.
The diagnosis is typically made when hair loss is observed.
The sample for examination includes hair from the affected area.
Bacterial swabs should be collected.
The swabs should be used for smear examination.
The swabs should be used for culture.
Wood's lamp (blacklight) examination will reveal a bright green to yellow-green fluorescence of hairs infected by Trichophyton mentagrophytes var.
Mentagrophytes, in kerion infection caused by Trichophyton verrucosum Invaded hairs show an ectothrix infection and fluorescence under Wood's ultra-violet light has been noted in cattle but not in humans.

Kerion has been called a great mimicker.
It can be easily confused with bacterial scalp abscess and various other conditions.
It may be misdiagnosed as smallpox.
A kerion can be mistaken for dissecting cellulitis of the scalp.

In a recent report, a previously well 9-year-old boy presented to the outpatients' clinic with a tender, swollen occipital scalp lesion progressing over one month's duration.
He was initially misdiagnosed as having a bacterial infection, and was given a 2 weeks' course of oral cephalexin.
No improvement was noticed after the course.

It was only after the scalp scrapings were examined, that the child was found to be having a local infection with the fungus Trichophyton tonsurans, which had resulted in kerion.
Antifungal therapy was then started, which resulted in complete cure.

Unlike most other manifestations of Tinea dermatophyte infections, Kerion is not sufficiently treated with topical antifungals and requires systemic therapy.
Typical therapy consists of oral antifungals, such as griseofulvin or terbinafine, for a sustained duration of at least 6 to 8 weeks depending on severity.
Successful treatment of kerion often requires empiric bacterial antibiotics given the high prevalence of secondary bacterial infection.

== Systemic antifungal therapy ==

Oral antifungal medication is used to treat tinea capitis.
Griseofulvin remains the first-line treatment and is for internal use only.
Tablets should be taken with a fatty meal, which facilitates absorption.
More recent antifungals that can be used include terbinafine, itraconazole, and fluconazole.
Itraconazole can be used as an alternative treatment.
The specific duration depends on the chosen molecule.
Antifungal medications are available for external use, such as imidazole compounds.

== Adjunctive and topical measures ==

A shampoo containing Ciclopirox olamine, such as Sebiprox, can be prescribed to increase the speed of fungal elimination.
These shampoos help reduce the risk of transmitting the infection to others.

== Management of inflammation ==

Steroids are used to reduce the severity of inflammation.
The administration of systemic corticosteroid therapy is controversial and is used in the treatment context.

== Wound care and procedural precautions ==

Scabs can be removed more easily using wet dressings or by spraying water.
Surgical incision and drainage should be avoided because performing it will lead to a prolonged or non-healing condition.

== Timing and prognosis ==

Appropriate treatment can be implemented quickly, and treatment must be started quickly.
Quick implementation of appropriate treatment limits the affected area and limits the size of the scar.
The majority of tinea capitis patients can be cured.

== Prevention ==

People should not share bedding or towels with others, nor touch pets such as cats or dogs, as touching pets can lead to infecting family members through animals.
Combs and hairbrushes should be disinfected or discarded.

== Other locations ==

Infection can occur in any other area exposed to fungi, such as the upper limbs, where it is known as tinea corporis.
