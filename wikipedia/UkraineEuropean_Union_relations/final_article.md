# UkraineEuropean Union relations

International relations between the European Union (EU) and Ukraine are shaped through the Ukraine–European Union Association Agreement and the Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Area (DCFTA). Ukraine is a priority partner within the Eastern Partnership and the European Neighbourhood Policy (ENP). The EU and Ukraine have been seeking an increasingly close relationship, going beyond co-operation, to gradual economic integration and deepening of political co-operation. On 23 June 2022, the European Council granted Ukraine the status of a candidate for accession to the European Union.

The association agreement was initiated in 2012, but the Ukrainian government suspended preparations for signing the association agreement on 21 November 2013, during the presidency of pro-Russian Viktor Yanukovych, who attended the EU summit in Vilnius on 28–29 November 2013, where the association agreement was originally planned to be signed but it was not. The decision to put off signing the association agreement led to the pro-EU Euromaidan movement. These led to the removal of Yanukovych and his government by parliament after the 2013–2014 Ukrainian revolution in February 2014.

The political part of the Association Agreement was signed on 21 March 2014, by the new Prime Minister, Arseniy Yatsenyuk. Meanwhile, the EU has attempted to stabilize Ukraine by freezing assets of allegedly corrupt Russians and Ukrainians and by granting financial aid to Ukraine. The economic part of the Ukraine–European Union Association Agreement was signed on 27 June 2014, by the new president, Petro Poroshenko. On 1 January 2016, Ukraine joined the DCFTA with the EU. Ukrainian citizens were granted visa-free travel to the Schengen Area for up to 90 days during any 180-day period on 11 June 2017, and the Association Agreement formally came into effect on 1 September 2017. On 21 February 2019, the Constitution of Ukraine was amended, the norms on the strategic course of Ukraine for membership in the European Union and NATO are enshrined in the preamble of the Basic Law, three articles and transitional provisions. On 28 February 2022, during the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy officially signed an EU membership application for Ukraine. Prime Ministers Mateusz Morawiecki of Poland, Janez Janša of Slovenia and Petr Fiala of the Czech Republic, all being member states of the European Union, visited Kyiv and met with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy to express their solidarity and support to Ukraine during the invasion.

Ukraine shares borders with four EU member states; Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, and Romania—that stretch about in total, with 33 border crossings by road, rail, ferry, and walking and bicycle path.

On 14 December 2023, the European Council agreed to open accession negotiations with Ukraine.

== European strategic preparation and criteria ==

The European Community began preparing for a new level of interaction in the mid-1980s, with this preparation aimed at creating a single Europe and the goal of this initiative being the creation of the European Union.
Active formation of unified European approaches to global issues began in the late 1980s, and these approaches included democratic transformations in socialist countries of Eastern Europe and the USSR as well as political dialogue with eastern neighbors.
The Western European Union welcomed the creation of the CIS on December 23, 1991, and confirmed its readiness to recognize the sovereignty of all new independent republics.
Recognition was conditional upon meeting specific criteria developed by the EU Council, one of which was the adoption of obligations regarding disarmament and non-proliferation of nuclear weapons.

== Russia's role and EU priorities ==

Russia was considered the main partner in relations with the international community among states that emerged on the territory of the former Soviet Union.
Russia was willing to act as the successor state to the Soviet Union, a willingness that received a positive assessment from the European Union.
The European community considered integration with Eastern European states, which were former participants in the socialist bloc, a priority.
The goal of stabilizing the situation on the European continent applied to the post-Soviet space in Europe and was achieved through the implementation of technical and financial assistance programs.
European politicians did not seek to make long-term political decisions regarding the former Soviet republics.

== Ukrainian diplomatic perspective and alignment ==

During the presidency of Leonid Kravchuk, Ukrainian Minister of Foreign Affairs Anatoliy Zlenko described in his memoirs that he felt the Berlin Wall had not disappeared for many Europeans, including politicians, but had merely moved to Ukraine's border.
The Verkhovna Rada of Ukraine adopted a decree on July 2, 1993.
Ukraine aligns with the statements and positions of the European Union on regional and international issues.

== Internal divisions and geopolitical orientation ==

Western Ukraine has intensive contacts with Poland, while the eastern and southern parts of the country have a high Russian population.
The industrialization of the Soviet Union led to a strong urbanization process in Eastern Ukraine, whereas Western Ukraine is still largely rural today.
There is a religious contrast between Eastern and Western Ukraine, as Eastern Ukraine is characterized by stronger atheistic tendencies and by Russian Orthodox or Ukrainian Orthodox faith, while Western Ukraine is characterized by Catholic, Uniate, or Ukrainian Orthodox faith.
The Orthodox churches in Eastern Ukraine recognize the Moscow Patriarchate, and the Orthodox churches in Western Ukraine recognize the Kyiv Patriarchate.
The country's internal political orientation involves a choice between Western orientation and close relations with Russia.
All presidential and parliamentary elections in Ukraine since its independence in 1991 showed a political division of the country.

Ukraine has long been seen as a critical but a difficult political partner of the European Union.
According to observers, this is due to such factors as the unwillingness of the EU to expand to the post-Soviet space, the poor performance of the Ukrainian economy, the lack of democracy (during the 1990s), and the internal instability (following the Orange Revolution).
Also, some experts notice the importance of the Russian factor in Ukraine–EU relations.

Ukraine's desire to join the European Union dates back to 1993 when the government declared that integration to the EU is the main foreign policy objective.
In reality, little was done since Kyiv had to take into account Russia, which remained its major trade partner and natural gas and fossil energy supplier.

The political dialogue between the EU and Ukraine started in 1994 when the Partnership and Cooperation Agreement (PCA) was signed.
That document was focused on economic and social issues and the necessity of conducting public administration reforms and guaranteeing free press and civil rights.
The framework set for political discussions was modest: a yearly meeting between EU Troika and Ukrainian leadership and some inter-ministerial consultations.
The first EU–Ukraine summit took place in September 1997 in Kyiv.
The Partnership and Cooperation Agreement of 1994 entered into force in 1998 and expired in 2008.
During the second summit in October 1998 in Vienna, EU–Ukraine relations were defined as a "strategic and unique partnership" and Ukraine first declared its desire to acquire associate membership in the EU.
None of the top-level meetings brought major changes to a reserved EU approach.
Leaders focused chiefly on post-communism economic transition and human rights records as well as on issues connected to the Chernobyl nuclear power plant and its containment.

In 2002, EU Enlargement Commissioner Günter Verheugen said that "a European perspective" for Ukraine does not necessarily mean membership within 10 to 20 years; however, it is a possibility.
The same year Ukrainian President Leonid Kuchma stated that Ukraine wanted to sign an association agreement with the EU by 2003–2004 and that his country would meet all EU membership requirements by 2007–2011.

== Recognition of independence and initial contacts ==

Relations between the European Community and Ukraine began in the early 1990s.
International relations between Ukraine and the European Union (EU) were established in December 1991, when the Soviet Union was dissolved.
The European Council Presidency was held by the Netherlands in the second half of 1991, and Hans van den Broek was the Dutch Minister of Foreign Affairs at that time.
Hans van den Broek formally recognized Ukraine's independence on behalf of the European Union, and the European Union officially recognized Ukraine's independence in December 1991.
The history of relations between Ukraine and the EU spans from 1991 to 2002.[307,308]
Ukraine declared independence on August 24, 1991, and the European Union recognized Ukraine in December 1991.
The European Commission issued a statement on December 2 and called for continued open and constructive dialogue with Ukraine.
Jacques Delors was the President of the European Commission on September 14, 1992, and Leonid Kravchuk was the first President of Ukraine on September 14, 1992.[358,359,360]

== Early policy challenges and nuclear issues ==

The EU's approach focused primarily on human rights and issues related to the Chernobyl nuclear power plant.
The European Community did not have full trust in Ukraine, as Ukraine was unwilling to immediately surrender Soviet nuclear weapons and unwilling to immediately join the non-proliferation treaty.
European policy towards Ukraine before 1994 was declaratory and extremely vague in its wording.
Russia is Europe's main partner in the post-Soviet space, and European politicians' concerns were reflected in the policy.
One concern was the Ukrainian leadership's unwillingness to compromise on nuclear weapons located on Ukrainian territory.
Another concern was the lack of progress regarding the shutdown of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant.
A further concern was the escalating confrontation between Ukraine and Russia.
High levels of economic instability in Ukraine were also a concern.
Ukraine joined the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty as a non-nuclear state.

== Ukrainian foreign policy and leadership changes ==

Ukraine's foreign policy in the early years of state independence was often described as 'multivectoral' by Ukrainian politicians, while foreign political observers often perceived this policy as inconsistent.
Ukraine and the EU sought closer ties with NATO and the EU in the 1990s, and good relations with Russia were of fundamental importance to Ukraine.
Kuchma said that Ukraine wanted to adopt economic restructuring plans, the goal of which was for Ukraine to achieve market economy status.
The election of Leonid Kuchma as President of Ukraine contributed to a shift in policy, as he was perceived in Europe as a young and energetic politician compared to Leonid Kravchuk, who was seen as a representative of the party nomenclature.
Leonid Kuchma engaged in active reformist activities and promised to reduce the level of confrontation in Russian-Ukrainian relations.
The policy of some European states towards independent Ukraine changed by the mid-1990s.
A decree titled 'On the Main Directions of Internal and Foreign Policy of Ukraine' established the priority of the European vector and expressed the aspiration to join the European Union.

== Shift in European approach in the mid-1990s ==

The situation changed in 1994.
In November 1994, German Chancellor Helmut Kohl named Russia and Ukraine as priority partners for Germany.
The European Commission did not give the Ukrainian leadership a clear answer regarding Ukraine's place in Europe.
The European Commission proposed to continue supporting democratic reforms in Ukraine and to develop partnership relations with Ukraine.
The Action Plan for Ukraine's concept generally repeated the provisions of the 1994 General Approach.

== The 1996 Action Plan and reform goals ==

The 1996 Action Plan for Ukraine stated that Ukraine was included in the sphere of European foreign policy interests and recognized as an independent actor in this context.
The document outlined reform paths for Ukraine with the goal of bringing the country closer to European standards, though it did not specify timelines for these reforms.
Main directions of assistance listed in the plan included support for economic reform, transformation of Ukrainian society, inclusion of Ukraine in the European security system, expansion of regional cooperation, deepening of contractual relations, and reform of the energy sector.
The Action Plan provided for an increase in the number of political contacts at all levels, included holding multilateral meetings, and called for expanding political dialogue on security issues.
It aimed to develop joint orientations with Ukraine in international relations based on common interests, proposed deepening contacts with the Western European Union, and proposed cooperation within the OSCE framework.
The goal was the gradual integration of Ukraine into the European security system.
The European Parliament approved the document in the spring of 1998.

The document did not specify the forms of the reforms and did not even mention the possibility of Ukraine joining the European Union.

== The 1999 Joint Strategy and strategic goals ==

The European Union adopted a joint strategy towards Ukraine on December 11, 1999.
The EU recognizes the significance of Ukraine's emergence as a democratic state and attaches great importance to Ukraine's pro-European orientation.
The strategy was planned for a duration of 4 years and could be extended beyond the initial 4-year period.
Strategic goals include supporting democracy and a free market, strengthening security and stability in Europe and the world, political cooperation particularly regarding Ukraine's approximation to the EU, cooperation in justice and home affairs, economic cooperation, and cultural cooperation.
Candidate countries for the Union were invited to participate in the strategy, and Poland was one of the candidate countries invited.

== Security cooperation and Petersberg missions ==

Ukraine was declared eligible to participate in the Petersberg missions, and nuclear security issues were discussed.
A consultation and cooperation mechanism covering crisis response was established between the EU and Ukraine.
Ukraine appointed a liaison for contacts with the EU Military Staff, and joint consultations and cooperation began.
The cooperation took place during the European Union Police Mission in Bosnia and Herzegovina (EUPM).

== Economic integration and structural reforms ==

The document contained provisions for supporting the country with structural reforms and economic reforms.
Ukraine was offered assistance in integrating into the European economy.
Brussels suggested sanctioning a status for Ukraine under the European Neighborhood Policy program.

== Democratic concerns and political dialogue ==

The European Union expressed support for reforms in Ukraine and supported Ukraine's aspirations to associate with the EU.
A joint statement was signed by Romano Prodi, the President of the European Commission, Jacques Chirac, the President of France, and Leonid Kuchma.
The opposition accused Leonid Kuchma of violating democratic principles.
In September 2001 and July 2002, EU leaders appealed to Ukraine to respect democratic principles.
An agreement was reached in which the European Union agreed to support Ukraine for the purpose of upholding democracy and justice, covering actions in the area of justice and regarding the respect of freedoms.
The second of these meetings jointly confirmed that Ukraine should adapt its legal system and standards to those of the EU.
Ukraine has sought to integrate into the European Union since then, a pursuit driven by the context of Eastern European expansion of the EU and NATO.
The European Commission presented a strategy paper on December 27, 2001.

The purpose of the paper was to shape the EU's relations with Ukraine while the European Community was awaiting its first eastern enlargement at the time.

== Early diplomatic contacts and strategic goals ==

Scholars believe the first Ukrainian president, Kravchuk, focused his foreign policy on Europe and that his diplomatic goal was to integrate into Europe.
Scholars also believe Ukraine's foreign policy leaned more towards the EU after the Cold War due to the relative strength of European forces compared to Russia.
Jacques Delors and Leonid Kravchuk met in Brussels on September 14, 1992, which was the first high-level encounter between the two countries.
During this meeting, Leonid Kravchuk expressed gratitude to the European Commission for its aid, and the two sides agreed to further develop their relations.[358,359,360] In April of the same year (1993), Kuchma announced at the Council of Europe summit that Ukraine's strategic goal was to integrate into the European structure and stated that the first step in this integration was to become a formal member of the EU.[356,357,361,362,363,364,365,366]

== Multi-vector foreign policy under Kuchma ==

Leonid Kuchma, the second Ukrainian president elected in 1993, hoped to change the one-sided policy and aimed to implement a 'multi-vector' foreign policy.[356,357,361,362,363,364,365,366]
By 2000, Kuchma stated that the three priority directions of Ukraine's foreign policy were the EU, Russia, and the United States, which he described as receiving sufficient attention and being mutually complementary.[356,357,361,362,363,364,365,366]
At his re-election inauguration in 1999, Kuchma again stated that Ukraine's strategic goal was to join the EU.[356,357,361,362,363,364,365,366]

== Democratic concerns and the Gongadze affair ==

Ukrainian journalist Georgiy Gongadze was murdered in September 2000.
The European Union considered the murder a major incident infringing on press freedom.
At the fifth EU-Ukraine summit in September 2001, the EU accused the Ukrainian government of hindering media and stated that the Ukrainian government's actions did not meet democratic standards.

== Kuchma's 2002 integration roadmap ==

In June 2002, Kuchma addressed the Ukrainian parliament, proposed a 10-year strategic concept for economic and social development in Ukraine from 2002 to 2011, and presented a timeline for joining the European Union for the first time.
The plan included negotiating a free trade area and becoming an EU partner country before 2004–2007, as well as establishing a customs union with the EU before 2005–2007.
Kuchma brought up the plan again at the summit.

== EU enlargement and the special status proposal ==

The European Union signed only one technology agreement with Ukraine.
Since 2001, Poland and other countries have suggested to the EU, which was then under the rotating presidency of Spain, that Josep Piqué offer Ukraine, Moldova, and Belarus an accession perspective as a proposed special status intended to incentivize reforms and economic changes in the three countries.[370,376] The proposal received support from Germany, some EU member states, and some candidate countries.[370,376] Ukraine reacted unenthusiastically to the proposal, and President Kuchma of Ukraine was disappointed by the lack of a prospect for membership in this status.[370,376] The European Union announced the list for its fifth enlargement in October 2002, after which Ukraine became a neighbor of the European Union.[370,384,385]

The Orange Revolution of late 2004 improved Ukraine's European prospects; the opposition leader Viktor Yushchenko hinted that he would press the EU for deeper ties and described a four-point plan: acknowledgment of Ukraine as a market economy, entry in the World Trade Organization, associate membership in the European Union, and, finally, full membership.
Ukrainian President Viktor Yushchenko asked Brussels mid-December 2004 for a clearer indication of Ukraine's prospects for membership, saying that "The approved Action Plan reflects only the level of Ukraine–EU relations that we could have reached before the presidential elections in 2004."

On 13 January 2005, the European Parliament almost unanimously (467 votes to 19 in favour) passed a motion stating the wish of the European Parliament to establish closer ties with Ukraine in view of the possibility of EU membership.
Though there is still a long way to go before negotiations about EU membership can start, the European Commission has stated that future EU membership will not be ruled out.
Yushchenko has responded to the apathetic mood of the commission by stating that he intends to send an application for EU membership "in the near future" and that he intends to scrutinise Ukraine's relationship with the Commonwealth of Independent States in order to assure that EU integration is possible, and, if not, to make it possible.
Several EU leaders have already stated strong support for closer economic ties with Ukraine, but have stopped short of direct support for such a bid.

On 21 March 2005, Polish Foreign Minister Adam Daniel Rotfeld noted that Poland will, in every way, promote Ukraine's desire to be integrated with the EU, achieve the status of a market-economy country, and join the World Trade Organization.
He also said, "At the present moment, we should talk concrete steps in cooperation instead of engaging in empty talk about European integration".
Three days later, a poll of the six largest EU nations conducted by a French research company showed that the European public would be more likely to accept Ukraine as a future EU member than any other country that is not currently an official candidate.

In October 2005, Commission president José Manuel Barroso said that the future of Ukraine is in the EU.
On 9 November 2005, however, the European Commission in a new strategy paper suggested that the current enlargement agenda (Croatia and in the future the other ex-Yugoslavian countries) could block the possibility of a future accession of Ukraine, Armenia, Belarus, Georgia, and Moldova.
Commissioner Olli Rehn said that the EU should avoid overexpansion, adding that the current enlargement agenda is already quite full.

According to the Ukrainian authorities, the ENP is not an adequate political instrument, since joining the EU was one of principal objectives of all governments since 1993.
After the Orange Revolution of 2004 that brought to power Viktor Yushchenko, the EU commission was very slow to react: little progress was made to put the largest European country on a path to eventual membership.

== Election crisis and international intervention ==

The Orange Revolution took place from November to December 2004 during the Ukrainian presidential election.
The first round of the election ended on October 31, 2004, and no candidate received more than 50% support in the first round.
On November 22, 2004, the Ukrainian Central Election Commission announced preliminary results for the second round of voting in the presidential election, in which Viktor Yanukovych was leading in the vote count.
Ukrainian citizens and international observers questioned the integrity of the election due to suspected fraud.
Supporters of Viktor Yushchenko flooded Maidan Nezalezhnosti in Kyiv.
The European Union did not express support for Yushchenko after the first round of elections, although Yushchenko was pro-European.
After the second round of elections, EU Commission President Barroso stated that the election process and results would be assessed seriously, objectively, and fairly, and that failure to conduct such an assessment would affect EU-Ukraine relations.
The observers questioned the election results and helped Yushchenko overturn the results of the second round of elections.
The EU and its member states sent thousands of observers who were dispatched through the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE).
On November 26, 2004, Polish President Aleksander Kwasniewski and Solana arrived in Kyiv.

On November 26, 2004, former Polish President Lech Walesa and Lithuanian President Adomas Adamkus arrived in Kyiv.
The group included the EU High Representative for Common Foreign and Security Policy, the President of Poland, the former President of Poland, and the President of Lithuania.
The delegation went to Kyiv to mediate.
Kuchma was forced to agree to reorganize the Central Election Commission and to amend the election law.
US President George W.
Bush made a phone call on December 8.
Yushchenko had won the election by that time.
Bush called Kwasniewski and Adamkus.
Bush thanked Poland and Lithuania for their assistance in the election.

== EU diplomatic response and election outcome ==

The European Union supported the opposition during the Orange Revolution and helped establish a round table, which resulted in a second round of presidential elections. The declaration was positively received, and the European Union looked favorably upon Kyiv at that time. The Brussels European Council meeting took place on December 16 to 17, after which the EU issued a statement. [390,391] In this statement, the EU congratulated the Ukrainian people and leaders for resolving the crisis with external help and expressed an intention to strengthen political, economic, and cultural ties between the two sides. [390,391] Viktor Yushchenko won the runoff election for president on December 26, 2004.

== Yushchenko's strategic vision and membership plans ==

Yushchenko proposed a four-step plan on December 10, 2004.
Yushchenko stated that Ukraine sought EU membership soon.
Yushchenko stated that Western orientation is Ukraine's strategic goal and that EU membership is associated with and part of this strategic goal.
Yushchenko gave a speech before the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe in Strasbourg on January 25, 2005.
Yushchenko gave a speech before the German Bundestag on January 25, 2005.
Yushchenko gave a speech before the German Bundestag on March 8, 2005.
The European Union sent contradictory messages at the end of 2005.
Yushchenko responded coldly to the statement.

In March 2007, the EU and Ukraine started talks about a new "wider agreement", aiming at offering a legal framework for a closer economic cooperation and a better political dialogue.
Negotiations began on March 5, 2007, with the goal of signing a new agreement to regulate mutual relations between Ukraine and the EU and to help Ukraine get closer to the EU.
The negotiations started during a conference in Paris and took place in Brussels and Kiev.
EU representatives declared that Europe is ready to start negotiations on creating a free trade zone between Ukraine and the EU and that they would discuss a new agreement between the parties.
The parties discussed cooperation in the energy sector and a common foreign and security policy.
EU representatives insisted on establishing strategic relations with Ukraine.
It was agreed that Ukraine and the EU would start a parallel negotiation concerning setting up a free trade area.
Later in 2007, it was announced that this issue would be incorporated into the draft agreement as a separate chapter.
The EU leaders proposed an association agreement.
A compromise was reached regarding the name of the agreement, which was named the Association Agreement.
An agreement for an Association Agreement was reached between Ukraine and the EU.
The proposed agreement was intended to include increased cooperation in the energy sector.
After the twelfth summit, both sides announced the start of negotiations on an Association Agreement.
Days before the summit, the Ministers of foreign affairs of Member States agreed during their meeting in Avignon (France) that association agreement to be signed with Ukraine will have nothing to do with the association agreements the EU signed with many Eastern European States (from Poland to Romania in the beginning of the 1990s, Western Balkans by the end of the 1990s).
Media reported that the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg and Germany were strongly opposed to including "Ukraine is a European State" into EU legally binding documents.
This was considered a failure of Kuchma-era politician Mr.
Roman Shpek, then Ukrainian ambassador to Brussels.
He was replaced by Mr.
Andriy Veselovsky, a more experienced diplomat.
A Joint EU–Ukraine Action Plan was endorsed by the European Council on 21 February 2005.
It was based on the Partnership and Cooperation Agreement of 1994 and provided, according to the European Commission, a comprehensive and ambitious framework for joint of work with Ukraine in all key areas of reform.
Talks on a free trade agreement between Ukraine and the European Union started on 18 February 2008 between the Ukrainian government and the EU Trade Commissioner.
Portugal publicly stated it supports Ukraine's EU accession in July 2008.
On 22 July 2008, it was announced that a "Stabilisation and Association" -type agreement would be signed between Ukraine and the EU on 8 September 2008 in Évian-les-Bains.

On 2 October 2008, Ukraine President Yushchenko announced that the Association agreement between the country and the EU would be signed "within six-eight months".
On that day, he met with the King of Sweden Charles XVI Gustav, who paid a state visit to Kyiv.
According to Yushchenko, "the agreement is half-ready, and he hopes that there will be a possibility to finalise and sign it under Swedish presidency in the EU".
He also welcomed the initiative of Eastern Partnership suggested earlier by Polish and Swedish Foreign Ministers.

On 20–24 October 2008, the EU and Ukraine held a negotiation round on the free trade area chapter of the Association agreement.
According to some Ukrainian media, the "EU promised to liberalise trade relations".
Ukraine's representative told that one must not "focus too much" on negotiation since there is much to be done by the Ukrainian government to meet certain criteria.
He also said, that "soon, the Balkans will enter into the European trade space and therefore Ukraine might lose these markets".
This was seen as a reason for Ukraine to move forward as least as soon as Balkans, the EU did not comment on that perception.

On 29 October 2008, the EU Commissioner Jacques Barrot and Ukrainian officials met in Brussels to launch negotiations on visa-free travel.
Kyiv had been asking for a "road map" to visa lifting, including travel document security, irregular migration, public order and foreign relations.
But the EU justice commissioner avoided to give any specific dates.
Moreover, the Ukrainian side argued that the 2007 visa facilitation agreement is not fully implemented by the EU member states.
The European Commission representative was quoted as saying that Brussels is ready to impose sanctions against those who do not respect the agreement.
Spanish, Dutch, German and Belgian embassies were cited among the most active rule breakers.
Making visa processing lengthy and expensive is one of the major agreement violations.
On 28 October 2008, Belgian PM Yves Leterme told that Ukrainians need to avoid middlemen in visa procedures if they want to reduce their cost.
The problem is that some consulates, including Belgian, oblige visa seekers to deal with a middleman.

According to Ukrainian President Yushchenko, some embassies of EU countries often require Ukrainians to present documents, which had not been foreseen in the agreement on simplification of visa regulations.
Around five per cent of Ukrainians willing to travel to the EU are denied visas, which, according to Yushchenko, "does not meet the standards of our agreements with the EU".

On 4 June 2009, some media outlets reported that Germany's Free Democratic Party openly stated in its programme that Ukraine has the right for the EU membership in the long term.
This was the first major German political party to state this.

On 16 June 2009, a new practical instrument was adopted – the EU-Ukraine Association Agenda.

In September 2009, high-ranking Ukrainian diplomats proposed that Ukraine apply for EU membership after the presidential election in January 2010, around March 2010, which would mean that the official response to the application would likely take place in early 2011 during the Polish presidency of the European Union.
However, this has not happened.

On 5 October 2009, the Chairman of the Committee of the Verkhovna Rada on issues of European integration Borys Tarasiuk commented "the EU see the implementation of a free visa regime for Ukrainians travelling to member states of the European Union only as a long-term prospect".
Ukrainian politicians continue to insist that the implementation of that free visa regime take place by 2012, when the European Football Championship will be held in Ukraine and Poland.
According to Tarasiuk, the main obstacles to the implementation of a free visa regime between Ukraine and EU is the fact that Ukraine "hasn't finished its work on legislation concerning forming a demographic [
database], which then could become a good basis for issuing biometric passports" and the fact that there is no general database on the issuing of foreign passports to Ukrainian citizens.
According to Tarasiuk the EU fear that this grants the opportunity for mass falsification.

On 16 December 2009, the European Commission President Jose Manuel Barroso stated "our Ukrainian friends need to do more if they want us to help them more."
He also stated that "enlargement is not possible in the current situation."

== Political instability and coalition shifts ==

The internal situation in Ukraine was deteriorating, which negatively affected contacts between Brussels and Kyiv.
A conflict arose between the presidential faction of Yushchenko and the Bloc of Yulia Tymoshenko, leading the two sides of the 'Orange coalition' to begin courting the opposition Party of Regions.
The Orange coalition was formed in this manner after parliamentary elections in 2007, but this situation led to the loss of the coalition majority in the Verkhovna Rada in June 2008.
The Party of Regions attempted to dismiss Tymoshenko's government on July 11, 2008, but the attempt failed.
The Bloc of Yulia Tymoshenko formed an alliance with the pro-Russian Party of Regions and the Communist Party of Ukraine.
The NU-LS party decided to leave the coalition, and Yushchenko made a decision on October 8 to dissolve the parliament.
The Bloc of Volodymyr Lytvyn came to the rescue of the coalition, and Volodymyr Lytvyn became the Chairman of the Verkhovna Rada.
Arseniy Yatsenyuk, who represented the NU-LS party, was removed from his position, while Tymoshenko remained the Prime Minister and led the coalition BYuT – NU-LS – Lytvyn Bloc.

== The 2008 gas crisis ==

Gas relations with Russia became a subject of disputes during the rule of the 'Orange Coalition'.
Gazprom demanded repayment of debts that were increasing due to the prevailing economic crisis.
Moscow exploited the Ukrainian political crisis and forced Ukraine to accept a gas price increase that would allow for increased revenue to the Russian budget.
This occurred in the context of a global financial crisis.
The parties failed to reach an agreement, which resulted in a gas war between Kyiv and Moscow.
The gas war involved the complete cutoff of gas supplies transported via a Gazprom pipeline passing through Ukrainian territory.
The gas crisis was resolved by an agreement signed after nearly three weeks.
Vladimir Putin signed the agreement as Prime Minister.

== Eastern Partnership and Association Agreement ==

On February 28, 2008, Yushchenko made a statement expecting his country to achieve associated EU membership status in the near future.
The concept was approved at an EU summit in June 2008.
Negotiations for an association agreement were announced on September 9, 2008, and the conference took place in September 2008.
The European Commission officially launched a proposal on December 3, 2008.

== Impact of the Russo-Georgian War ==

The Russo-Georgian War occurred in August 2008 and was a negative factor for the Ukrainian political scene.
Part of the political class supported Russia, while part of the political class and part of the society supported Georgia.

Ukraine is one of six post-Soviet nations to be invited to cooperate with the EU within the new multilateral framework that the Eastern Partnership is expected to establish.
However, Kyiv pointed out that it remains pessimistic about the "added value" of this initiative.
Indeed, Ukraine and the EU have already started the negotiations on new, enhanced political and free-trade agreements (Association and Free-Trade Agreements).
Also, there has been some progress in liberalising the visa regime despite persistent problems in the EU Member States' visa approach towards Ukrainians.

That is why Ukraine has a specific view of the Eastern Partnership project.
According to the Ukrainian presidency, it should correspond, in case of his country, to the strategic foreign policy objective, i.e. the integration with the EU.
Yet, the Eastern Partnership documents (the European Council Declaration of May 2009) do not confirm such priorities as political and economic integration or lifting visas.

Ukraine has expressed enthusiasm about the project.
Ukraine deputy premier Hryhorii Nemyria said that the project is the way to modernise the country and that they welcome the Eastern Partnership policy, because it uses 'de facto' the same instruments as for EU candidates.

Under the Eastern Partnership, Poland and Ukraine have reached a new agreement replacing visas with simplified permits for Ukrainians residing within 30 km of the border.
Up to 1.5 million people may benefit from this agreement, which took effect on 1 July 2009.

== Launch and participation ==

Ukraine has been a member of the Eastern Partnership since 2009 and joined the initiative on May 7, 2009.
The negotiations took place in Paris, where the Caucasus crisis was a background factor, and Ukraine and the EU decided to start negotiations on September 9, 2008.
The agreement was originally scheduled to be signed by the end of 2009.
The Eastern Partnership Declaration was signed on May 8, 2009, with participation from 27 European Union countries and heads of government or ministers from six Eurasian countries.
The Eastern Partnership plan officially began after the declaration was signed.

== Strategic goals and scope ==

The European Union supported developing cooperation within the Eastern Partnership, the main goal of which is to create necessary conditions for accelerating political and economic integration between the European Union and interested partner countries.
The plan aimed to deepen cooperation between the EU and Ukraine and included specific areas of cooperation such as energy, transport, the environment, and visa facilitation.

== Political and security cooperation ==

The political part of the Agreement covers political interaction, security issues, and counter-terrorism.
The signed document stipulates that political dialogue must take place within the framework of the association council and be conducted at the level of foreign ministers and experts.
The agreement includes provisions for cooperation in foreign policy, partnership for resolving regional conflicts, and Ukraine's participation in EU civilian and military operations to overcome crisis situations as well as in EU exercises and training.

== Legislative alignment and legal obligations ==

The agreement requires Ukraine to adopt approximately 60 percent of the European Union's law, including regulations on energy, technical regulations, sanitary regulations, phytosanitary regulations, customs regulations, and regulations on intellectual property protection.
The agreement will be implemented in two stages involving gradually aligning Ukrainian legislation with EU requirements and standards.

== Economic integration and trade facilitation ==

The DCFTA is the most important part of an association agreement signed in June 2014.
The agreement was intended to secure the flow of capital and to create equal competitive conditions.
The agreement addresses computer reservation systems, environmental protection, and consumer rights.
Signing this agreement will allow domestic exporters to mark their products with the CE mark, sell their products freely in the EU market, and do so without additional certification.
Products marked with the European CE conformity mark can be imported into Ukraine and sold in the Ukrainian market without additional certification.
This will reduce smuggling and simplify life for importers in Ukraine.

According to the European commission paper, trade with Ukraine is dominated by manufactured goods.
