# Lightvessel Gedser Rev

Lightvessel No. XVII Gedser Rev (Danish: Fyrskib XVII Gedser Rev) is a decommissioned lightvessel built in 1895, now serving as a museum ship in Helsingør, Denmark, having formerly been stationed in the Nyhavn Canal in Copenhagen. It is owned by the National Museum and takes its name after Gedser Rev south of Falster where it was stationed most of its working life.

Denmark's first lightvessel was built at Jacob Holm's shipyard at Christianshavn in 1829, where Jacob Holm lived from 1770 to 1845.
Built at N.F.
Hansen's shipyard in Odense in 1895, the Gedser Rev was number seventeen in the line of Danish lightvessels, as all Danish lighthouse ships were numbered sequentially.
It was first stationed at Lappegrund in shallow waters at the entrance to the Øresund, which is located northwest of Helsingør.
It was powered by two steam engines which were replaced by a 16-hp kerosene engine in 1918.
In 1921, a new three-cylinder Voelund 135-hp propulsion engine was installed, a spark-ignition engine was installed, and the ship was moved to a position at Gedser Rev, south of Falster, itself the southernmost point of Denmark.
The ship was equipped with radio direction finding, an electric underwater mine signal (VMS), and a radio beacon.
The lighthouse ship Gedser Rev was towed into place.
The crew consisted of 14 people and was divided into two shifts.
Each shift worked one month on board and was followed by one month of leave.
In 1940, when Denmark was occupied by Germany in World War II, the German occupying forces confiscated the ship and placed it near Kalundborg, but it returned to its old position in 1945 after the war had ended.
The ship was involved in a number of collisions during her years in operation.
The most serious of these occurred in 1954 when she sank within a few minutes on the port side of the vessel.
The seaman on duty was thrown overboard and drowned while the rest of the crew were saved.
The lighthouse vessel was salvaged and repaired.
The lighthouse vessel remained at that position until 1972.
During the Cold War and after the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961, many East Germans chose to escape by water, heading North to Denmark.
These refugees crossed the Baltic Sea.
Although most failed and many died in the attempt, at least 50 were rescued by the Gedser Rev.
As the southernmost limit of Danish territory and as an obviously recognisable target, many aimed for the lightship.
One notable escapee was Manfred Burmeister in 1969, who escaped by aid of a petrol-driven submersible scooter.
The ships of the Warsaw Pact were reported and photographed.

XVII was decommissioned in 1972 and put up for sale at the lightship warehouse at Holmen in Copenhagen.

The donation amount was NOK 8,122,686.
AP Møller and his wife Chastine Mc-Kinney Møller are the donors.
The donation funded a comprehensive renovation of the lighthouse.

The lightvessel's regular home for several years has been the Nyhavn Canal in Copenhagen, Denmark where was open to the public Saturdays from 11 am to 3 pm from June through August.
It was maintained by a group of volunteers.

On 9 May 2018 it was towed from Nyhavn to dock 2 in Helsingør harbor, to be exhibited there for five years.

== Notable events ==

The escape route was across the Baltic Sea, and the escape took place in September 1969.
Bernd Böttger was from Sebnitz and used a self-built underwater scooter to pull himself through the Baltic Sea.
Manfred Burmeister was a Berlin engineer and successfully escaped in October 1969.

== Crew history ==

The great-grandfather served as a lookout and on the fire ship.

On 27 May 2009 Bank of Denmark issued a new 20 krone coin with lightvessel XVII, as depicted by the artist Karin Lorentzen, on its reverse.
The coin was part of a ship series that had been issued since 2007, and each coin in the series has a face value of 20 kr.

== Cultural legacy ==

Jakob Bodo Schultz is a guitar maker from Bornholm who made an electric guitar for an Australian musician from an old piece of ship planking that came from the fire vessel 2013.
