# Cyclobutane

Cyclobutane is a cycloalkane and organic compound with the formula (CH₂)₄. Cyclobutane is a colourless gas and is commercially available as a liquefied gas. Derivatives of cyclobutane are called cyclobutanes. Cyclobutane itself is of no commercial or biological significance, but more complex derivatives are important in biology and biotechnology.

The bond angles between carbon atoms are significantly strained and as such have lower bond energies than related linear or unstrained hydrocarbons, e.g. butane or cyclohexane.
As such, cyclobutane is unstable above about 500 °C.

The four carbon atoms in cyclobutane are not coplanar; instead, the ring typically adopts a folded or "puckered" conformation.
This implies that the C-C-C angle is less than 90°.
One of the carbon atoms makes a 25° angle with the plane formed by the other three carbons.
In this way, some of the eclipsing interactions are reduced.

== Conformational Geometry ==

One form reduces the steric strain of the molecule.
The conformation balances the effects of angular strain and torsional strain.
A planar conformation of the cyclobutane molecule would result in torsional strain because eight hydrogen atoms would be eclipsed relative to each other.
The bond angles deviate from 109 degrees and range down to 88 degrees.
The molecular skeleton is a slightly folded rhombus.
This planar square structure deviates significantly from the ideal bond angle for sp³ hybridized carbon atoms, which is 109.5 degrees, whereas in a planar square structure the bond angles are 90 degrees.
Torsional strain is caused by eclipsing hydrogens, and in the planar conformation the hydrogen atoms would be exactly eclipsed.
The folding occurs around the ring's diagonals and happens along one diagonal or the other in succession.
The carbons move alternatively above or below the plane.
The carbon skeleton arrangement is approximately 80 degrees and cyclobutane forms bent bonds.
Eclipsed interactions between hydrogen atoms on adjacent carbon atoms are largely avoided, though some hydrogen interactions are eclipsed.

The bond angles between carbon atoms in a cyclobutane molecule differ significantly from the tetrahedral angle of 109.5 degrees, which is energetically optimal, and the actual bond angles in cyclobutane are approximately 88 degrees.
The explanation for the bond angle deviation is related to hydrogen atom conformation, as if the bond angle were 90 degrees, the hydrogen atoms would be in an eclipsed conformation relative to each other where steric interactions increase the molecule's energy.
Instead, the hydrogen atoms are slightly staggered relative to each other, and these hydrogen interactions are reduced [cite: 32].
The bond angle in the ring is 88 degrees, the angle between the C(1)C(2)C(4) plane and the C(2)C(3)C(4) plane is approximately 35 degrees, and in this conformation, the torsion angle between C-H bonds is approximately 25 degrees.

== Dynamic Behavior ==

The cyclobutane molecule is non-rigid and undergoes pseudorotation, during which carbon atoms move alternately out of the plane.
The energy barrier that must be overcome for cyclobutane to invert via the planar transition state is very low, with a value of approximately 6.1 kJ/mol.
The peak of the potential energy curve corresponds to a planar conformation.
In substituted derivatives, the two conformations are not equal, though the energy difference between the two states is small.

== Chemical reactions ==

Decomposition occurs at temperatures greater than or equal to 420 degrees Celsius, a process called pyrolysis that follows a unimolecular kinetic law and splits the substance into two molecules of ethylene.
The decomposition product of cyclobutane at 500 °C is ethylene.
These reactions are not replicable on cyclopentane or higher cycloalkanes, which are affected by some ring strain that is significantly less than that in the original molecule.
Unsubstituted cyclobutane does not easily undergo ring-opening reactions.
Cyclobutane exhibits typical alkane reactions, including substitution reactions that follow a free radical mechanism.
Cyclobutane reacts with chlorine in the presence of light to form chlorocyclobutane.
Hydrogenation occurs at elevated temperatures, during which the cycle opens to produce butane.
Cyclobutane undergoes addition with chain scission.

Cyclobutane can be hydrogenated to butane using palladium, platinum, or nickel as the catalyst.
The reaction proceeds at 200 degrees Celsius when using nickel and at 50 degrees Celsius when using platinum.
Cyclobutane undergoes combustion reactions and reacts with fluorine gas to produce octafluorocyclobutane.
