# Ītzpāpālōtl

In Aztec religion, Ītzpāpalōtl ("Obsidian Butterfly") was a striking skeletal warrior and death goddess and queen of the Tzitzimimeh "star demons", who ruled over the paradise world of Tamōhuānchān, the paradise of victims of infant mortality and the place identified as where humans were created. She is the mother of Mixcoatl and is particularly associated with the moth Rothschildia orizaba from the family Saturniidae. Some of her associations are birds and fire. However, she primarily appears in the form of the Obsidian Butterfly.

Itzpapalotl's name can either mean "obsidian butterfly" or "clawed butterfly"; the latter meaning seems most likely.
It's quite possible that clawed butterfly refers to the bat and in some instances Itzpapalotl is depicted with bat wings.
However, she can also appear with clear butterfly or eagle attributes.
Her wings are obsidian or tecpatl (flint) knife tipped.
(In the Manuscript of 1558, Itzpapalotl is described as having "blossomed into the white flint, and they took the white and wrapped it in a bundle.")
She could appear in the form of a beautiful, seductive woman or terrible goddess with a skeletal head and butterfly wings supplied with stone blades.
Although the identity remains inconclusive, the Zapotec deity named Goddess 2J by Alfonso Caso and Ignacio Bernal may be a Classic Zapotec form of Itzpapalotl.
In many instances Goddess 2J, whose image is found on ceramic urns, is identified with bats.
"In folklore, bats are sometimes called "black butterflies"".
Itzpapalotl is sometimes represented as a goddess with flowing hair holding a trophy leg.
The femur is thought by some scholars to have significance as a war trophy or a sacred object in Pre-Hispanic art.

== Names and Symbolic Meaning ==

Itzpapálotl is also known as Itzpapalocíhuatl.
The name combines two important Mesoamerican symbols: the butterfly, called papalote, and obsidian, called itztli.
Obsidian was a symbol of blood, which represented war and sacrifice.
The name symbolized the metamorphosis and rebirth of Aztec warriors.
The numen represents that the strength and great power possessed are found in magic.

== Hybrid Animal Attributes ==

Butterfly wings were attributed to her nahualli, and Itzpapapalotl was represented as a dragon with butterfly wings.
It is highly probable that "Bat-winged Butterfly" refers to a bat, while Itzpapalotl can be represented as a light-colored butterfly.
She often appeared among people in the form of a Rothschildia Orizaba butterfly, and the entity was depicted as a butterfly known today as Rothschildia orizaba orizaba.

== Claws and Limbs ==

Its hands and feet are replaced by jaguar claws, and it possesses jaguar-like curved claws.
It was depicted with large jaguar paws, and its hand fingers are jaguar claws.
The woman had jaguar claws, and the woman figure had jaguar claws on her hands.
The entity had jaguar claws on her hands in this depiction.
Its foot toes are eagle claws, and the woman figure had eagle claws on her feet.
The entity had eagle talons on her feet in this depiction.

== Facial Features and Demeanor ==

The goddess has a gloomy demeanor.
The entity did not have a tongue and had a knife instead of a tongue.

== Attire and Adornment ==

The goddess was supposed to wear white powder as makeup and various colored cosmetics, and her appearance resembled that of a lady from the Mexican Court.
Her breastplate represents power and fire, and it has feathers arranged like blades.
The woman wore a hip belt.

== Depictions and Artifacts ==

An image featuring a butterfly wing with a stone knife blade attached to a skull head first appeared in early Postclassic Tula.
Itzpapalotl was depicted as a woman holding an obsidian knife and was often depicted on ceramic urns.
This imagery gives the goddess a symbol of rebirth and regeneration.
This feature defines it as a tzitzimitl.

Itzpapalotl is the patron of the day and associated with the stars Cozcuauhtli and Trecena 1 House in the Aztec calendar.
The Trecena 1 House is one of the five western trecena dates dedicated to the cihuateteo, or women who had died in childbirth.
Not only was Itzpapalotl considered one of the cihuateteo herself, but she was also one of the tzitzimime, star demons that threatened to devour people during solar eclipses.
She was the patron of the 16th day of the month, the name of which is Cozcacuauhtli.
Her naualem is a deer.
Calli (house) is associated with the ancestral homeland of humanity.
It was believed that a person born on her day would have good health, a long life, and a happy life.
In Mesoamerica, the habit of moths and butterflies flying into fire was considered a heroic act of self-sacrifice.
Butterflies themselves were also seen as symbols of fire.
Warriors wore images of butterflies during combat.
Sacrifices were made to appease this goddess.
The sacrifices consisted of corn tortillas shaped like a butterfly.
One of the prominent aspects of the ritual surrounding Itzpapalotl relates to the creation story of the Chichimec.
The ritual is illustrated in the sixteenth century document known as the Map of Cuauhtinchan No.
2.
An illustration from this document shows Chichimec warriors emerging out of a seven-chambered cave behind Itzpapalotl.
The deity is shown brandishing a severed leg, thought to be a symbol of battle.
Beginning in the 1990s, archeologists exploring the Barranca Del Aguila region, southwest of Mexico City, have discovered caves carved to simulate the seven chambered cave, known as Chicomoztoc, from the ritual creation narrative.

According to the Manuscript of 1558, section VII, Itzpapalotl was one of two divine 2-headed doe-deers (the other one being Chimalman) who temporarily transformed themselves into women in order to seduce men.
Itzpapalotl approached the two "cloud serpents named Xiuhnel and Mimich ", who transformed themselves into men (so as to disguise themselves when all the others of the Centzonmimixcoa had been slain in the ambush?).
To Xiuhnel, Itzpapalotl said "'Drink, Xiuhnel.'
Xiuhnel drank the blood and then immediately lay down with her.
Suddenly she ... devoured him, tore open his breast. ...
Then Mimich ... ran and ... descended into a thorny barrel cactus, fell into it, and the woman fell down after him."
In the myth-history narrative of the Annales de Cuauhtitlan, the cloud deity victims take the form of deer, the hearts of whom are eaten by Itzpapalotl.
The theme of the heart devouring goddess appears in other global mythologies.

== The Chichimeca Hunt and Death of Itzpapálotl ==

Legend states that Itzpapálotl was sent to Earth wearing a cloak that granted her invisibility.
The Annals of Cuauhtitlan narrate this event.
When the Chichimecas arrived, they were guided by four hundred Mixcoas.
During their journey, the Chichimecas and their guides fell into the power of Itzpapálotl.
Itzpapálotl ordered the Chichimecas to shoot a red tiger, a red snake, and a rabbit.
Iztac Mixcóatl escaped from their hands and hid inside a water caltrop.
Iztac Mixcóatl summoned the four hundred Mixcoas who had died.
Itzpapálotl attacked the biznaga.
Iztac Mixcóatl emerged quickly and shot repeatedly at the biznaga.
They shot her repeatedly as a result.
Itzpapálotl died and was burned.
The warriors covered themselves with her ashes and painted dark circles under their eyes using the ashes.

The text from the Annals of Cuauhtitlan, a manuscript with pre-Hispanic origins written with European characters, states that they killed Itzpapálotl and worshipped her as a goddess after killing her.

== Cosmic Origins and Celestial Transformation ==

Itzpapálotl became a mocihuaquetzqui after her death, and a mocihuaquetzqui is one of the stars that accompany the sun.
The gods who ruled the Milky Way created the stars, the gods, and humans, and Itzpapálotl was the daughter of the gods who ruled the Milky Way.
The souls of dead warriors were believed to become butterflies or other flying creatures, and these transformed souls were thought to live in the sun's paradise.
It was believed that Itzpapalotl was the lady of souls of the dead transformed into stars.

== Birth of the Obsidian Knife and Demigods ==

According to legend, Itzpapalotl gave birth to an obsidian knife from which earthly demigods were born.
Mictlantecuhtli picked up the white object, which turned out to be an obsidian knife for sacrifice, wrapped it in cloth, and has carried the knife with him ever since.
Mixcoatl picked up the white stone, bound the knife with cloth, and has carried the knife with him ever since.

== The Hunt of the Centzon Mimihtoa ==

According to legend, two of the Centzon Mimihtoa, who are the Four Hundred Northern Stars and gods of the northern starry sky, went on a hunt.
A pair of double-headed deer descended from the sky in front of them during the hunt.
Another woman, who was Itzpapalotl, tried to convince Mimich to eat.
Mimich built a fire.
A cactus fell from the sky.
The cactus blocked her path.
She mourned Mimich.
The fire god heard Icpapalotl's lamentations.
The fire god threw her into a bonfire.
She disintegrated into five multicolored stones in the bonfire.
The cloak allowed her to transform into a harmless butterfly.
She combed her hair.
She painted her face white.

The fire god heard the lamentation of Icpapalotl and threw her into a bonfire, where she disintegrated into five multicolored stones.

== The Battle with Mictlan and Final Incineration ==

The goddess was the first woman to be ritually sacrificed and was incinerated during her ritual sacrifice.
Itzpapálotl ate the four hundred mixcoas, and according to one myth, she captured and ate Ceoton-Mimixcoa.
Mictlan called the spirits of the slain gods and defeated Icpapalotl together with those spirits.
Mictlan burned Icpapalotl's body afterwards, and the death of the Goddess was caused by war.
She sometimes appeared in the form of a deer and wore a magical cloak that made her invisible.

== Cultural origins and spread ==

Itzpapálotl is one of the main goddesses of Chichimeca culture and one of the most important goddesses of Chichimeca culture.
Itzpapalotl was worshipped by the Chichimec Indians.
It is highly probable that Ixcpalotl originated as a Chichimec hunting deity, and the deity was originally one of the Chichimec hunting gods.
Her cult spread among the Nahua peoples, which included the Acolhua, the Tlaxcaltecs, and the Aztecs.

== Roles and attributes ==

Ītzpāpālōtl is a goddess of war, the mother of war, and the mother of human sacrifices.
She is the patroness of death and a deity of earth, agriculture, and death.
Ītzpāpālōtl is the goddess of fire and stars and the night spirit of blazing stars.
She is a goddess of fate and is associated with the cult of plants.
Ītzpāpālōtl was worshipped as Mother Earth and symbolized cyclical changes in nature.
She embodied the creative forces of nature and symbolized the potential for life.
Ītzpāpālōtl was a symbol of hope for posthumous rebirth.
It was especially believed she was the lady of women who died during childbirth, and she is a symbol of the souls of women who died in childbirth.
The power of Itzpapálotl is directed toward wise and elderly women.
This archetype represents a witch, mage, or sorceress.

== Associated deities and concepts ==

Tamoanchan is a mythical place and an earthly paradise.
Cihuateteo are one of the star demons, and these spirits mischieved at night and on special days.
In Aztec culture, the obsidian knife was a symbol of vitality and soul.
Itzpapalotl had a male counterpart, and the male counterpart of Itzpapalotl was IItztlacoliuhqui.
This representation symbolized human fear of death.
Tamocanchan rules over the heavenly realm.
The star demons were destined to destroy humanity in the future.
