# Amak Volcano

Amak Volcano is a basaltic andesite stratovolcano in the Aleutian Islands of Alaska, United States, from Anchorage. It is located on the eponymous island, from Frosty Volcano and near the edge of the Alaskan Peninsula's western flank. Only boats are allowed to access the island with a certain permit.

Blocky (dotted with flat blocks of minerals and crystals) lava flows stream from its summit to its flanks. Three historical eruptions have taken place – two within the 18th century, the first from 1700 to 1710, and the latter in 1796. The earliest prehistoric eruption was believed to have taken place between 3050 and 2050 BCE.

Cold Bay, the city nearest Amak, is easily accessible by plane.
Amak is accessible only by boat; airplanes are not permitted to land on the island.
Access to the island is restricted to small boats.
Private boat rides to Amak are available in Cold Bay, but for access to the Aleutian Islands, a permit is required from the United States Fish and Wildlife Service.
Mount Amak is 995 km (618 miles) from Anchorage, Alaska.

Amak Island lies in the Bering Sea, north of the main archipelago of the Aleutians.
It is one of two islands (along with Bogoslof Island) that are north of the main range.

The United States has the most active volcanoes in the world, many of them geologically young.
In Alaska, at least 50 volcanoes, including those in the Aleutian archipelago, have erupted in historical time.
The state accounts for ~80% of the United States' volcanoes, excluding the seamounts in the area, ~8% of world volcanoes, and most of these are located among the Aleutian Islands.
The Aleutian Islands arc serves as the northern boundary of the Pacific Ring of Fire, where tectonic activity generates earthquakes and volcanic eruptions in masses.

The volcano is basaltic-andesitic in composition.
It is a modest stratovolcano, rising no more than above sea level.
The volcanic crater is distinct, and has erupted in historical times, only "blocky" lava flows.
Charles Wood and Jürgen Kienle, volcanologists, propose that earlier activity, 4,000–5,000 years ago, consisted primarily of lavas of ethereal (fine) platy and thick andesite.
Amak Volcano is unique in that its andesitic lavas, while composed the same as the other Aleutians, contain an abundance of potash.
They also could contain more sodium carbonate and rare-earth element deposits than the Aleutian norm.
Between Bogoslof, the other Aleutian island north of the main arc, and Amak, Amak's lavas are more alkalic and silicic.

Glaciation took place around the volcano roughly 6700 years BP, carving out U-shaped valleys.
At the southwest flank of the island, a crater, likely a maar, can be found amid an alluvial plain.

== Location and regional context ==

Amak Island is 31 miles (50 km) north of the main range and situated in the northern part of the Alaskan Peninsula.[2,3]
The volcano is located north of the Alaska Peninsula and in Bristol Bay.
Mount Amak is the easternmost of the Aleutian Islands.
The volcano is 50 km (31 miles) from Frosty Volcano and 50 km northwest of another volcano.
These two islands are located 50 km (31 miles) north of the main Aleutian Islands and 50 km north of the main island chain.[2,3][2,3]

== Topography and dimensions ==

Mount Amak is the highest point of Amak Island and has a conical shape. [5,6]
The volcano rises to 513 meters above sea level, has a height of less than 513 meters (1,683 feet) above sea level, and has an elevation of 448 meters. [1,8][1,8]
Amak has a height of 488 meters and an elevation of 488 meters.
The volcano occupies an area of 1 square kilometer and has a total volume of approximately 1 cubic kilometer.
A flat plain with a ridge is found on the southwestern coast of the island, and the crater is located in the center of an alluvial plain.

== Geological structure and composition ==

Mount Amak is part of the Aleutian Range and is considered a relatively young volcano that was formed during the Pleistocene epoch.
The volcano is constructed of many layers formed from lava, tephra, pumice, and volcanic ash, and it is very similar to a large volcanic dome.
This volcano has a moderate steep lava layer, and its lava flows were primarily composed of andesite.
Amak lava contains abundant ash salts, and the lava flow contained blocky fragments of mineral and crystal flat powder with a bulbous shape.
On the south-western flank of the island, there is a crater with a flat bottom and a distinctly overturned rim.

== Naming history ==

Mikhail Teybenkov named the island and volcano for the first time in 1852.

The Amak Volcano has erupted three times in historical times: circa 2550 B.C., from 1700 to 1710, and in 1796; the first of these events was identified with tephrochronology.
Each eruption has been characterized by lava flows and lava clumps, and the two most recent eruptions included a crater eruption.
Volcanic activity occurred primarily from the summit crater.

== Monitoring and research ==

Mount Amak is associated with the Global Volcanism Program, and the source of the description is the Alaska Volcano Observatory.
The information comes from the Global Volcanism Program and is from the United States Geological Survey.
A report was published in 2006 by the United States Geological Survey, which defined Volcano Amak as a very high priority target among the Aleutian Islands volcanoes.
