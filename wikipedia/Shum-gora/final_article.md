# Shum-gora

Shum Gora () is a massive kurgan (tumulus) situated in Peredolskaya Volost, near the bank of the Luga River, Batetsky District, Novgorod Oblast, northwestern Russia, about 60 km west of Novgorod.

The hill was formerly involved in local liturgical practice. 19th century sources record that three crosses standing on its top, but by the 20th century these had been removed. During the mid 19th century, there used to be processions, with pilgrims walking three times around the hill before ascending it to "listen to the noise" and leave small sacrifices in a pit at the top. People suffering from headache used to put sand from the pit in their ears. These practices are described by A. A. Panchenko in 1998. The site and the associated folk customs were first described by M. Bystrov in 1879. Bystrov records the tradition that the sand from the hill had miraculous power on Trinity Sunday, and that if the hill was ascended on that day, the sound of bells could be heard if one "listened in a special way", and that on this day, pilgrims from as far as 50 miles away would come to the hill in hope of curing headaches.

The kurgan has not been excavated, but in 2002 a georadar survey was performed by the Russian Federal Geological Institute (ВСЕГЕИ), and in 2003 to 2004, the Institute for the History of Material Culture of the Russian Academy of Sciences has done experimental surveys testing non-intrusive archaeological methods. The kurgan is 14.6 m in height and 70 m in diameter, comparable in proportion to the largest Migration era tumuli in Scandinavia such as Raknehaugen.

Aleksashin (2006) discusses a boulder with a monogrammatic inscription he found on the hill in 2003. He compares the inscription to Carolingian monograms and based on this revives the theory which identifies Rurik, the founder of the Kievan Rus, with Rorik of Dorestad.

== Location and setting ==

Shum-Gora is located on the left bank of the Luga River and between the villages of Podgorye and Zapolye.
The location is in a marshy part of the field, does not dominate the surrounding terrain, and offers a poor view from the river.
An old cemetery begins to the east, is located 50 meters away, and is situated in a forest.
The mound is part of the Oredezh mounds complex, which stretches for kilometers and is located in the interfluve of the Luga and Oredezh rivers.
A third side featured an image of a 'double-tooth' or 'trident', a symbol associated with the Rurikids.

== Physical description ==

Shum-Gora is a sacred burial monument and one of the largest medieval burials in Rus.
The object is part of a large archaeological complex that includes a settlement mound (gorodishche), a village site (selishche), and a group of hills (sopki).
The mound is distinguished from the surrounding hills by its dimensions, with a first tier height of 6.5 to 8 meters, a second tier base diameter between 34 and 35 meters, and an upper platform diameter reaching 18 meters.
The surface is paved with pieces of limestone flint driven into the ground of the slopes in the form of terraces and covered with a layer of topsoil 10 to 20 centimeters thick.
The upper platform of the mound is flat, and this flatness is similar to that of the Gnezdovo Scandinavian mounds.
Research revealed a chamber inside an artificial mound that is clearly oriented from east to west, measures 9 meters by 3 meters by 3 meters, and is located at the center of the mound.

The chamber is situated 14 to 15 meters below the summit.
A wide passage leads to the chamber and starts from the heel stone of the burial mound.
Items could be carried into the passage on stretchers via the heel stone.
Boulders are present along the perimeter of the base.

== Archaeological findings ==

Shum-Gora dates from the 8th to the 10th centuries.
Two stone slabs were discovered in 2003 and were decorated with monograms (galdrastaf).
One side of the monograms resembled signs on Charlemagne's obols, while another side resembled signs on Charles the Bald's Verden denarii (840–877 AD), and another side resembled the monogram of the Byzantine Palaiologos dynasty.
Modern archaeological findings and laboratory analysis of artifacts support the historical account.
The artifacts were discovered during excavations at Ryurikovo Gorodische near Novgorod.
A wooden Varangian fortress was built at this site and served as a prince's residence.
According to M.
N.
Tikhomirov, Rurik may have built this fortress, which was constructed between 858 and 861 AD.
The collected material, combined with the shape and size of the Cross Hill, resembles similar 'royal barrows' found in Swedish and Danish burials.

== Rituals and traditions ==

The processions took place annually and occurred during the week of All Saints, starting from the Church of Saint Great-Martyr George with participants including local residents and priests to attend a prayer service at the site.
On Parental Saturdays, the deceased were commemorated.
The first detailed description of the remains of the Peredolsky pogost was published in 1880 and included the 'Big Hill' (Bolshaya Sopka).
Local residents prayed at the cross during the week of All Saints' Day, held a memorial service at the site, and made a religious procession to the top of the hill on All Saints' Day.
Three crosses stood on the summit before the stone cross appeared and were possibly named after Rurik, Sineus, and Truvor.
Trinity Sunday is a suitable day for announcing a divinely chosen monarch to the people and serves as a symbol of divine blessing upon royal (princely) authority.
Folk rituals were conducted on the 'Krestovaya Sopka' hill during the Trinity holiday, likely preserved a trace of some state ritual, and indicated the important place of Rurik's burial mound in the destiny of Russia.
