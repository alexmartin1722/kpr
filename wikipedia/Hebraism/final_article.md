# Hebraism

Hebraism is a lexical item, usage or trait characteristic of the Hebrew language. By successive extension it is often applied to the Jewish people, their faith, national ideology or culture.

A Hebraism is a word formation or expression that contradicts the rules of a language when a word or expression is translated too literally from Hebrew.
Such a stylistic figure is generally called a barbarism.
Hebrew has many idiomatic terms that are not easily translatable to other languages, for example בארבע עיניים be'arba enayim, literally 'with four eyes,' means face to face without the presence of a third person, as in, 'The two men met with four eyes.'
The writer David Bivin cites 'be'arba enayim' as an example.
The expression לא דובים ולא יער lo dubim ve lo ya'ar is literally "neither bears nor forest" but means that something is completely false.
The saying טמן את ידו בצלחת taman et yado batsalakhat "buried his hand in the dish" means that someone idles away his time.
The Statenvertaling contains many Hebraisms.
These Hebraisms often take the form of the Hebrew genitive.
A well-known example of Hebrewisms in the Statenvertaling is the expression 'heilige der heiligen'.

"Hebraism" may also refer to a lexical item with Hebrew etymology, i.e. that (ultimately) derives from Hebrew.
For example, the English word stiff-necked, meaning "stubborn", is a calque of Greek σκληροτράχηλος, which is a calque of Hebrew קשה עורף qeshēh ʿōref "hard of neck; stubborn".
Similar calques are the way of women (דרך נשים) "menstruation" and flowing with milk and honey (זבת חלב ודבש) "abundance".

Sometimes Hebraisms can be coined using non-Hebrew structure.
For example, the Yiddish lexical item ישיבה בחור yeshive bokher, meaning "Yeshivah student", uses a Germanic structure but two Hebrew lexical items.

== Hebraisms in the Spanish language ==

According to David Gonzalo Maeso, approximately fifty Hebrew words have entered the Spanish language and can be grouped into four main areas.
The first area includes direct Hebrew words incorporated into common language and religious life, such as Yahveh (Jehovah), aleluya, amen, Bethlehem, donkey, Kabbalah, Caraites, hosanna, Jebusite, Jubilee, kibbutz, Ladino, Leviathan, Masoretic, Maccabee, Messiah, Moabite, Eden, Pharisee, manna, Onanism, Passover, Rabbi, Sabbath, Sefardi, shekel, sodomy, Talmud, and Torah.
The second area includes numerous Biblical Hebrew personal names, some of which are well-known central biblical characters with names ending in the particle '-el', including Samuel, Ismael, Gabriel, Rafael, Daniel, and Satan.
Less common biblical names include Ana, Bartolomé, Benjamin, Bernabé, Manuel, María, Miguel, Susana, Tadeo, and Tomás.
Hebrew formulas have been incorporated into the Spanish language, including superlatives such as 'King of Kings' or 'Song of Songs' and constructions consisting of repeating a word three times, such as 'Holy, Holy, Holy is the Lord of the Universe'.
In these formulas, placing an article before an adjective is a Hebrew way of constructing a phrase, exemplified in Spanish by saying 'la pequeña' when referring to several sisters.
It is common to speak of a 'Salomonic' ruling, which refers to a just sentence, and to refer to the youngest brother as 'the Benjamin'.
The meanings of non-Hebrew words such as 'sin' (pecado) and 'to bless' (bendecir) are derived from Hebrew or the Bible.

Examples of such words include 'holy' (santo), 'just' (justo), 'blessed' (bienaventurado), 'baptism' (bautismo), 'wicked' (impío), 'brother' (hermano), 'earthly' (terrenal), 'heavenly' (celestial), 'to multiply' (multiplicarse), and 'Lord' (Señor).
The word 'pan' is mentioned as an example and has a generic sense referring to food.

Czech as a spoken language was influenced very little by Hebrew, with this influence occurring mostly through Vulgar Latin.

== Translation of prepositions and scriptural citations ==

Mark 1:2 states, "Behold, I send my messenger before thy face," while Malachi 3:1 states, "a messenger to prepare the way before me," and Mark cites or paraphrases Malachi.
The Czech translation attempts to capture the actual Hebrew meaning in Malachi ("before") and the Hebraism in Mark's Greek ("before thy face").
This happens with terms that have multiple different meanings in the target language.

== Semantic distinctions in Gospel terminology ==

The term 'psyche' (ψυχὴ) is found frequently in the Gospels, where it generally means 'life' and does not specifically mean 'soul'.
Modern Czech translations attempt to account for this distinction, an approach that always comes at the expense of any potential ambiguity in the original text.

== Hebraism in Philology and Translation ==

In philology, Hebraism is a term used to describe a literal translation that refers to the opening phrase of a new story, with examples including "and it happened" or "it has been done." The Dutch translation "heilige der heiligen" renders the Hebrew genitive form, which represents the superlative degree, meaning "the holiest," a usage well-known in Proverbs 1:2.
The Statenvertaling contains many Hebraisms because the translators tried to follow the source text as accurately as possible rather than to write as pure Dutch as possible, resulting in a text that contained duplications such as the phrase regarding Sara's life.

Finally, the word "hebraism" describes a quality, character, nature, or method of thought, or system of religion attributed to the Hebrew people.
It is in this sense that Matthew Arnold (1869) contrasted Hebraism with Hellenism, identifying Thomas Carlyle as his age's embodiment of the former.

== Historical Context of Hebraism ==

Alexander the Great launched an eastern expedition, after which Greek culture spread widely from the Mediterranean to Asia west of India, permeated and circulated throughout these regions, and fused with Asian cultures to create Hellenistic culture.
The legacy of Hellenistic culture was inherited during the era of ancient Rome, which had seized hegemony from Greece (the Kingdom of Macedon).
Within this historical flow, the cultural nature of Judaism and Christianity gradually permeated Europe through figures such as Philo of Alexandria (a Jew), the apostles of Christianity, Greek Church Fathers, and Latin Church Fathers, and Judaism and Christianity were absorbed as part of Hellenistic culture.
Christianity did not require adherence to Jewish ethnicity or strict observance of religious laws as a prerequisite, actively proselytized and preached to non-Jews, and spread significantly despite various persecutions.
In 313, the Edict of Milan granted Christianity official recognition within the Roman Empire, and in 392, Christianity was established as the sole state religion of the Roman Empire.
Islam was established in 610, and while Christianity had a certain presence in Asia (the Middle East), the spread of Islam covered the Middle East with 'Hebraism' different from European Christianity.
