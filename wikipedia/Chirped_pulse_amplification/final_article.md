# Chirped pulse amplification

Chirped pulse amplification (CPA) is a technique for amplifying an ultrashort laser pulse up to the petawatt level, with the laser pulse being stretched out temporally and spectrally, then amplified, and then compressed again. The stretching and compression uses devices that ensure that the different color components of the pulse travel different distances.

CPA for lasers was introduced by Donna Strickland and Gérard Mourou at the University of Rochester in the mid-1980s, work for which they received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2018.

CPA is the current state-of-the-art technique used by most of the highest-power lasers in the world.

Before the introduction of CPA in the mid-1980s, the peak power of laser pulses was limited because a laser pulse at intensities of gigawatts per square centimeter causes serious damage to the gain medium through nonlinear processes such as self-focusing.
For example, some of the most powerful compressed CPA laser beams, even in an unfocused large aperture (after exiting the compression grating) can exceed intensities of 700 GW/cm², which if allowed to propagate in air or the laser gain medium would instantly self-focus and form a plasma or cause filament propagation, both of which would ruin the original beam's desirable qualities and could even cause back-reflection potentially damaging the laser's components.
In order to keep the intensity of laser pulses below the threshold of the nonlinear effects, the laser systems had to be large and expensive, and the peak power of laser pulses was limited to the high gigawatt level or terawatt level for very large multi-beam facilities.

In CPA, on the other hand, an ultrashort laser pulse is stretched out in time prior to introducing it to the gain medium using a pair of gratings that are arranged so that the low-frequency component of the laser pulse travels a shorter path than the high-frequency component does.
After going through the grating pair, the laser pulse becomes positively chirped, that is, the high-frequency component lags behind the low-frequency component, and has longer pulse duration than the original by a factor of 1000 to .

Then the stretched pulse, whose intensity is sufficiently low compared with the intensity limit of gigawatts per square centimeter, is safely introduced to the gain medium and amplified by a factor of a million or more.
Finally, the amplified laser pulse is recompressed back to the original pulse width through reversal of the process of stretching, achieving orders-of-magnitude higher peak power than laser systems could generate before the invention of CPA.

In addition to the higher peak power, CPA makes it possible to miniaturize laser systems (the compressor being the biggest part).
A compact high-power laser, known as a tabletop terawatt laser (T³ laser, typically delivering of energy within ), can be created based on the CPA technique.

== Q-switching ==

The technique of light self-injection contributes to renewed interest in Gain Switching.
Light self-injection enables pulse narrowing through external compression techniques.
Q-switching can be achieved by incorporating an attenuator inside the laser cavity, and any type of attenuator can be used for this purpose.
Light exiting the amplifier medium does not pass through the attenuator again, and there is no laser amplification.
Attenuation inside the laser cavity corresponds to a reduction in the cavity's Q factor.
A high Q factor corresponds to a laser cavity with low losses per round trip, while a low Q factor corresponds to a laser cavity with many losses.
A device used for this purpose is called a Q-switch.
The active medium is initially pumped, and the Q-switch is adjusted to prevent cavity amplification through the active medium.
The cavity has a low Q factor as a result of this adjustment, and laser emission cannot occur without the cavity operating.
The process produces a population inversion in the medium.
The rate of stimulated emission depends on the amount of light entering the medium.
The amount of energy accumulated in the active medium increases while it is being pumped.
Stimulated emission causes energy losses, and other processes cause energy losses.
After a certain period of time, the accumulated energy reaches a maximum level.

When the accumulated energy reaches its maximum level, it is said that the gain has saturated.
The Q-switch device is switched rapidly at this point, which minimizes attenuation and causes the cavity quality factor to increase considerably.
As a result, the laser cavity begins to function properly and the optical amplification process via stimulated emission begins.
A large amount of energy is accumulated in the active medium, the light intensity inside the cavity increases very rapidly, and the stored energy in the medium decreases almost completely and very rapidly.
This pulse is also known as a giant pulse, which can have a very high peak intensity.

== Mode-locking ==

A typical laser emits continuously, whereas a mode-locked laser emits a series of short pulses that occur at a constant repetition frequency.
In the frequency domain, a mode-locking laser emits in a series of spectral lines that are separated by the laser's repetition frequency.
The basic technique involves inducing a phase relationship between the modes of the laser in the resonant cavity.
A resonant mode exists if the light field amplitude reproduces itself after one round trip in the cavity.
The length of a longitudinal mode is defined by the formula L* = n * L and is equal to q times C divided by 2 times nu_q.
The value of g is generally large for common lasers, and the length L is much greater than the wavelength lambda.
This method uses coupling between longitudinal modes of the laser that have spectral spacing.
This method uses a laser with sufficiently broad emission bands that contain a large number of longitudinal modes.
This value is equal to C divided by 2 times L star.
Different longitudinal modes generally have a random phase relationship with each other, and the coherent sum of these modes results in a relatively low average intensity.
The modes can be synchronized to have a common phase in some areas of space.

If the modes are synchronized, the coherent sum will result in a pulse with higher power.
The pulses are periodic, and their period is equal to 1 divided by delta nu sub L and also equal to 2 times L star divided by C.
This value corresponds to the actual travel time of the pulse as it travels inside a cavity.
The resulting pulse is the sum of N longitudinal modes, and the width of the pulse will be determined by this summation.
The symbol tau is equal to one divided by N times delta nu, equal to T divided by N, and equal to one divided by delta nu.
Delta nu represents the spectral width, which refers to the laser emission line.
Ultra-short pulse laser systems operate on the femtosecond scale and use mode-locking methods.
Mode-locking is performed in active media with a broad emission bandwidth, such as Titanium:Sapphire (Ti:Za).
In practice, synchronizing modes consists of modulating losses within the cavity at the frequency Δν_L, causing longitudinal modes to tend to oscillate in phase.
Active switches are used to achieve this.

Passive switches are used to achieve this, and a Kerr-Lenz switch and a saturable absorber are examples of passive switches.
An acousto-optic modulator is an example of an active switch.
Passive mode-locking technique does not require an external signal, and the laser produces pulses without an external signal in passive mode-locking.
The most common type of device is a saturable absorber, which is placed in a laser cavity.
The device behaves differently depending on the intensity of light passing through it, and the ideal saturable absorber selectively absorbs low-intensity light and transmits light of sufficient intensity.
The saturable absorber attenuates low-intensity light from the wave, which leads to absorption of low-intensity light and selective amplification of high-intensity peaks.
The process occurs after many trips in the cavity, and this process results in a very powerful pulse train.
The method consists of phase modulation, which allows increasing or decreasing the instantaneous frequency.
The instantaneous frequency is denoted as sweep frequency or chirp.

There are several ways to construct compressors and stretchers.
However, a typical Ti:sapphire-based chirped-pulse amplifier requires that the pulses are stretched to several hundred picoseconds, which means that the different wavelength components must experience about 10 cm difference in path length.
The most practical way to achieve this is with grating-based stretchers and compressors.
Stretchers and compressors are characterized by their dispersion.
With negative dispersion, light with higher frequencies (shorter wavelengths) takes less time to travel through the device than light with lower frequencies (longer wavelengths).
With positive dispersion, it is the other way around.
In a CPA, the dispersions of the stretcher and compressor must cancel out.
Because of practical considerations, the (high-power) compressor is usually designed with negative dispersion, and the (low-power) stretcher is therefore designed with positive dispersion.
Different spectral components are brought back into phase in the compressor.
The output pulse has zero dispersion.

In principle, the dispersion of an optical device is a function \tau(\omega), where \tau is the time delay experienced by a frequency component \omega.
(Sometimes the phase \phi(\omega)=2\pi\tau(\omega)c/\lambda(\omega) is used, where is the speed of light and \lambda is the wavelength.)

It turns out to be hard to tune the dispersions of the stretcher and compressor such that the resulting pulses are shorter than about 100 femtoseconds.

== Principles of parametric amplification ==

OPCPA stands for Optical parametric chirped-pulse amplification.
Crystalline materials without inversion symmetry can exhibit nonlinearity, which allows for parametric amplification.
In this process, pump photons are converted into lower-energy signal photons and idler photons.
The crystal material does not heat up during this process.
Parametric processes are sensitive to the optical phases of waves, so efficient conversion requires phase matching within the range of wavelengths of interest.
The bandwidth of the gain curve is strongly determined by the width of the phase matching condition, which depends on chromatic dispersion.
Parametric amplification is possible over a wide range of wavelengths, and under optimal phase matching conditions, the gain bandwidth is very broad.

== Advantages of OPCPA systems ==

The technique Optical Parametric Chirped Pulse Amplification (OPCPA) is used for amplifying ultrashort pulses and is one of the most prominent alternatives when a greater amplification bandwidth is sometimes required.
CPA can also be used for optical parametric amplifiers (OPAs), and using CPA with OPAs results in an OPCPA laser.
OPCPA systems benefit from a significant reduction in peak intensities at high energies, which is achieved through temporal amplification of chirped pulses.
Increasing the pulse duration to the order of nanoseconds allows for higher pump energies, and higher pump energies result in higher amplified pulse energies.
It is no longer necessary to use ultra-short pulses for pumping, so Q-switch lasers can be used for pumping in the nanosecond regime.
OPCPA requires fewer amplification stages and most often requires only one amplification stage, which allows for high-energy pulses lasting only a few femtoseconds.
Thermal effects in the crystal are much less than those in a laser amplifier because there is less heat in the crystal due to weak absorption.
High beam quality can be achieved for the amplified pulses, and this result is obtained by combining previous factors with high efficiency.

== Technical challenges and limitations ==

It is difficult to satisfy the phase matching condition, high-quality pump beams are required, and most available nonlinear crystals have small apertures.
