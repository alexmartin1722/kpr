# IsraelUganda relations

Israel–Uganda relations refers to the current and historical relationship between Israel and Uganda. Neither country has a resident ambassador. Uganda has a non-resident ambassador in Cairo.

In 1903, the British Uganda Programme proposed Uganda as a Homeland for the Jewish people.

Under Milton Obote, Uganda helped Israel support rebels in Southern Sudan during their long war with the north.
At some point Obote wanted to make peace with the Khartoum government and cut off support to the rebels.
When Idi Amin overthrew Obote in 1971, he restarted support for the rebels and continued the military relationship with Israel.

Amin visited Israel in 1971 and was toasted by Israeli Defence Minister Moshe Dayan.
The Israelis were responsible for supplying and training much of the Ugandan Army and for undertaking several construction projects in the country.
But in February 1972, Amin suddenly decided to visit Libya (while traveling on an Israeli jet) and meet with leader Colonel Gaddafi.
After the visit, Amin became much more vocally anti-Israel.

By March 1972, Amin had ordered all Israelis expelled from Uganda.
One report says Amin became enraged over Israel's refusal to supply Uganda with jets for a war with neighboring Tanzania.
Amin became an outspoken critic of Israel.
By the end of the month, all the Israelis had gone, including some who had driven all their valuable construction equipment across the border into Kenya.

In 2016, an Israeli company was chosen to make a national master plan for the development of Uganda, on the basis of the Israeli National Outline Plan system, namely National Outline Plan 35.

== The Uganda Programme (1903–1905) ==

In 1903, Joseph Chamberlain, the British Colonial Secretary, presented a proposal to Theodor Herzl, the founder of the Zionist movement, for land located on the Mau Plateau.
The proposed territory covered approximately 12,950 square kilometers and belongs to Kenya today [cite: 1,2].
At the time, Palestine was part of the Ottoman Empire, and a Hebrew state was not considered feasible there [cite: 19,20].
Herzl met with Chamberlain in 1903 and asked him to allocate a territory from the British Empire [cite: 19].
Herzl's idea was received with enthusiasm at the sixth Zionist Congress [cite: 19,20].
Some Zionist activists considered founding a temporary Hebrew state in Uganda.
Three commissioners were sent by the Zionist Congress to examine the suitability of the offered territory in 1905 [cite: 1,2].
Two of the commissioners spoke against the colonization plan [cite: 1,2].
The Zionist Congress rejected the offer [cite: 1,2].
Herzl's idea was eventually rejected within a few months [cite: 19,20].
A majority of Zionist activists rejected the idea and refused to establish a Jewish state anywhere other than on the biblical land [cite: 19,20].

== Private arms brokering ==

Amos Golani, a former commander of Unit Tzomet (Cherry Unit), was one of the businessmen involved in arms trading with Uganda and founded a company with two partners who had connections in Uganda.

Golan's business partners sued him in 2002, alleging that he systematically avoided paying them their share of the deals.

== Migrant deportation controversy ==

In the summer of 2013, it was revealed that Israel planned to deport migrants and asylum seekers from Africa to Uganda.
The Ugandan government denied signing an agreement with Israel for the reception of migrants.
Israel began transferring migrants and asylum seekers from Africa to Uganda at the beginning of 2014.
The transfers were conducted secretly, and the migrants and asylum seekers had signed an Israeli 'voluntary departure' agreement.
The transfers were made in exchange for financial payment.
The Ugandan government continued to deny having an agreement with Israel and claimed that the transfer of migrants by Israel was not done with its consent.
In April 2018, Israel requested to forcibly deport asylum seekers to Uganda.
Israel stated it had not signed an agreement with Uganda to accept them.

Operation Entebbe was a counter-terrorism hostage-rescue mission carried out by the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) at Entebbe Airport in Uganda on the night of 3 July and early morning of 4 July 1976.
In the wake of the hijacking of Air France Flight 139 and the hijackers' threats to kill the hostages if their prisoner release demands were not met, a plan was drawn up to airlift the hostages to safety.
These plans took into account the likelihood of armed resistance from Ugandan military troops.
Originally codenamed Operation Thunderbolt by the IDF, the operation was retroactively renamed Operation Yonatan in memory of the Sayeret Matkal commander Lieutenant Colonel Yonatan "Yoni" Netanyahu, who was killed by a Ugandan sniper.
Three hostages, seven hijackers, and 45 Ugandan soldiers were killed.
In addition to the death of Netanyahu, five other Israeli commandos were wounded.
A fourth hostage was killed by Ugandan army officers at a nearby hospital.

== Hijacking details and flight route ==

The hijacking occurred on June 27, 1976, involving an Air France Airbus A300 that was connecting Tel Aviv to Paris with a stopover in Athens.
The aircraft carried 258 passengers and 12 crew members, though another account states there were 246 passengers and 12 crew members on board.
A Palestinian-German terrorist group consisting of four terrorists hijacked the plane after it departed from Athens.
The hijackers initially diverted the aircraft to Benghazi, Libya, where the plane landed first, before flying it to Entebbe Airport.
The terrorists demanded the release of 53 pro-Palestinian prisoners.

The peak of hostility involved the hijacking of an Airbus A300 aircraft belonging to the French airline Air France on a flight route from Ben Gurion Airport to Paris.
The aircraft refueled with kerosene in Benghazi, departed towards southern Africa, had not planned a destination at that time, and circled for more than an hour.

== Ugandan involvement and hostage separation ==

Ugandan President Idi Amin personally greeted the terrorists, and local Ugandan authorities supported the hostage-takers.
The Jewish hostages were taken to the old transit hall of the terminal, where they were separated from the other captives who were left free.
It is commonly accepted that Amin Dada had no connection to the hijackers, although he sympathized with the Palestine Liberation Organization, had previously presented himself as a mediator, and was not informed about the arrival of the hijacked plane until the last moment.

== Operation execution and casualties ==

The Israeli commandos arrived without being detected and used descriptions of the airport provided by the 147 hostages released by the kidnappers.
Three hostages were killed during exchanges of fire, while almost all of the remaining hostages were released and evacuated aboard three Israeli Hercules C-130 aircraft.
Three accomplices had joined the original hostage-takers.
Yonathan Netanyahu, the brother of future Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, was involved in the operation.
Almost all hostages were released, though one Israeli hostage was not released because she was hospitalized at that time.
A large portion of its fighter jets were destroyed during the operation.
The hostage's daughter who was hospitalized was killed after the operation, an act ordered by Amin.
Almost all hostages were rescued as a result of the operation, with 102 out of 106 captured hostages freed.
The fourth hostage's name was Dora Bloch.

== Destruction of Ugandan Air Force ==

The Israeli command destroyed eleven fighter jets that were parked on the airfield, an action intended to protect the departure of the freed hostages. These destroyed jets represented one quarter of the entire Ugandan Air Force. Eleven Ugandan aircraft were destroyed on the ground by the raid, which was intended to avoid the risk of being pursued. The Ugandan Air Force was severely damaged.

== Fate of Dora Bloch ==

Dora Bloch was an Israeli hostage who suffered from a serious medical condition before being evacuated a few days earlier to a Ugandan hospital.
At the time of her death, Dora Bloch was in a hospital.
Dora Bloch was assassinated the day after her evacuation.

The Abayudaya are a Jewish community of Baganda people in eastern Uganda who live in several villages near the town of Mbale.
They are devout in their religious practices, keeping Shabbat and keeping kosher.
The community is composed of converts and the descendants of converts.
The majority of the Abayudaya are recognized by the Reform and Conservative sects of Judaism.
However, the villagers of Putti are seeking an Orthodox conversion and observe Orthodox Judaism.
Their population is estimated at approximately 1,100, having once been as large as 3,000 (prior to the persecutions of the Idi Amin regime); like their neighbors, they are subsistence farmers.
Most Abayudaya are of Bagwere origin, except for those from Namutumba who are Basoga.
They speak Luganda, Lusoga or Lugwere, although some have learned Hebrew as well.

In November 2017 Chabad opened a permanent Jewish center in Uganda, becoming the 100th country to host a Chabad center.

== Origins and early history ==

Semei Kakungulu, a warlord from the Mbale region in East Uganda, founded a religious movement called the Abayudaya that was largely inspired by Judaism, adopted the Hebrew calendar, and founded several synagogues [cite: 21].
These ancestors adopted the Jewish faith in 1919 [cite: 1].
Semei Kakungulu died in 1928, after which the Abayudaya movement continued to flourish but remained isolated from Jews in the rest of the world [cite: 21].
The Abayudaya community's transition to Judaism began in the early 20th century under the patronage of American rabbis of the Conservative movement [cite: 50].

== Demographics and location ==

The Abayudaya community, also known as the Ugandan Gers [cite: 50], lives mainly in Nabugoya near Mbale and in Pallisa.
The Abayudaya are an analogue to the Children of Israel but are not ethnically Jewish and are not historically Jewish.
The Abayudaya were specifically targeted as an ethnic group not of his origin [cite: 25].
