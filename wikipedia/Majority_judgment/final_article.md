# Majority judgment

Majority judgment (MJ) is a single-winner voting system proposed in 2010 by Michel Balinski and Rida Laraki. It is a kind of highest median rule, a cardinal voting system that elects the candidate with the highest median rating.

== History and development ==

The majority judgment was invented by two French researchers, Michel Balinski and Rida Laraki, who work at the Centre national de la recherche scientifique (CNRS).
The theory was published in 2007 in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
Michel Balinski and Rida Laraki gave a course at the Collège de France that was part of seminars organized by Pierre Rosanvallon on the theme of the modern and contemporary history of politics.

Voters grade as many of the candidates as they wish with regard to their suitability for office according to a series of grades.
Balinski and Laraki suggest the options "Excellent, Very Good, Good, Acceptable, Poor, or Reject," but any scale can be used (e.g. the common letter grade scale).
Voters can assign the same grade to multiple candidates.

As with all highest median voting rules, the candidate with the highest median grade is declared winner.
If more than one candidate has the same median grade, majority judgment breaks the tie by removing (one-by-one) any grades equal to the shared median grade from each tied candidate's column.
This procedure is repeated until only one of the tied candidates is found to have the highest median grade.

== Voter evaluation and ballot design ==

The determination is based on predicates assigned by voters to candidates, with examples including 'Excellent', 'Good', 'Acceptable', 'Poor', and 'Bad'.
Candidates are ranked according to their predicates, which possess a strict, transitive total ordering among themselves that includes a uniquely 'best' predicate, a uniquely 'second-best' predicate, and a uniquely 'worst' predicate.
Voting under Majority Judgment proceeds similarly to rating-based voting where the determination is based on the submitted ratings and each voter assigns exactly one score to each candidate.
If a voter prefers candidate x over y, they will assign a higher score to x [cite: 3].
Voters assign a grade to each candidate in this method [cite: 4], and Majority Judgment uses verbal rating scales instead of numerical ones [cite: 7].
The acceptions of these grades are more homogeneous among voters [cite: 7].
Voters are asked to evaluate candidates by assigning grades such as 'very good', 'good', 'fairly good', 'passable', 'insufficient', or 'to be rejected'.
Voters can express disagreement with one or all candidates through these labels instead of abstaining, casting a blank vote, or an invalid vote.
Voters can express themselves more nuancedly if the proposed scale of values is fine-grained.
The voter assigns a verbal rating to each candidate, and the scale must be common to all voters.
This requirement leads to the terminology 'common language' in their work.

They insist that the ballot paper must explicitly ask voters to answer a specific question.
An example question asks voters to judge if a candidate would be suitable for leading France.
A specific question must be asked for every election and for every electoral system.
Without this specific question, each voter answers their own personal questions, and in this scenario, the sum of votes has no meaning.

== Determination of the majority mention ==

A 'majority mention' is determined for each candidate.
The majority mention is the unique option that receives an absolute majority of votes against any lower-ranked option.
The majority mention is the unique option that receives an absolute majority or a tie against any higher-ranked option.
The number of voters is assumed to be ordered according to the rating given to the candidate, from worst to best.
When the number of voters is odd (of the form 2N+1), the majority mention is the one assigned by voter N+1.
When the number of voters is even (of the form 2N), the majority mention is the one assigned by voter N.

This selection method means that an absolute majority (strictly more than 50%) of voters judge that a candidate deserves at least their majority mention and that half or more (50% or more) of voters judge that a candidate deserves at most their majority mention.
This concept resembles the notion of a median.

== Tie-breaking procedures ==

When a tie occurs, it is assumed that the median score for all tied candidates is alpha [cite: 3].
One 'Good' rating is removed from each candidate.
Majority Judgment selects a winner based on the predicate supported by an absolute majority, where the candidate with the best predicate is selected and the best predicate supported by an absolute majority determines the winner.
If no winner is found, a random draw is conducted [cite: 3].

== Mechanics of the median and consistency ==

The voting procedure assigns the same predicate to a candidate for both groups, and the candidate must also be assigned that same predicate for the total electorate.
The electorate is divided into two parts, V1 and V2, where the candidate has been assigned the predicate P in both parts.
The proportion of voters in V1 who prefer the candidate over others is less than 50% of the size of V1, and the proportion of voters in V1 who prefer others over the candidate is less than or equal to 50% of the size of V1.
The proportion of voters in V2 who prefer the candidate over others is less than 50% of the size of V2, and the proportion of voters in V2 who prefer others over the candidate is less than or equal to 50% of the size of V2.
The set of voters rating the candidate better than P is the union of two disjoint sets V1 and V2, where the number of voters in V1 rating the candidate better than P is less than 50% of |V1| and the number of voters in V2 rating the candidate better than P is less than 50% of |V2|.
Consequently, the number of voters from the total electorate who rated the candidate better than P is less than half, and at most half of the voters in the total electorate rated the candidate worse than P.
Majority Judgment assigns the predicate P to the candidate, and this assignment applies to the entire electorate.
Expressing hatred has the same impact on the result as simple disapproval, and the impact of a vote on the result depends only on where the evaluation stands relative to the final median.

Unlike score voting, majority judgment can have no-show paradoxes, situations where a candidate loses because they won "too many votes". In other words, adding votes that rank a candidate higher than their opponent can still cause this candidate to lose.

In their 2010 book, Balinski and Laraki demonstrate that the only join-consistent methods are point-summing methods, a slight generalization of score voting that includes positional voting. Specifically, their result shows the only methods satisfying the slightly stronger consistency criterion have:

\sum_{\text{vote} \in \text{ballots}} f(\text{score}_\text{vote})

Where f is a monotonic function. Moreover, any method satisfying both participation and either stepwise-continuity or the Archimedean property is a point-summing method.

This result is closely related to and relies on the Von Neumann–Morgenstern utility theorem and Harsanyi's utilitarian theorem, two critical results in social choice theory and decision theory used to characterize the conditions for rational choice.

Despite this result, Balinski and Laraki claim that participation failures would be rare in practice for majority judgment.

In arguing for majority judgment, Balinski and Laraki (the system's inventors) prove highest median rules are the most "strategy-resistant" system, in the sense that they minimize the share of the electorate with an incentive to be dishonest.
However, some writers have disputed the significance of these results, as they do not apply in cases of imperfect information or collusion between voters.
Strategically inciting a candidate in the opposing camp to split their votes no longer offers any strategic advantage.

== Frequency of ties and the median concept ==

A tie in scores between two candidates is rare, yet it is frequent that several candidates have the same majority mention.
Having the same majority mention is known as the notion of median.
Because there are more candidates than available grades, it is inevitable that multiple candidates receive the same majority grade.

== Tie-breaking procedure and median voter removal ==

The issue of tie-breaking is important, and the goal of majority judgment in a tie is to distinguish the candidate who is closest to having another mention.
This distinction is made based on that other mention, and the method removes the vote of the voter who determined the majority mention to break ties among candidates with the same majority mention.
This voter corresponds to the median voter when the number of voters is odd.
The new majority mention is determined for these candidates after removing the vote, and a candidate loses against other candidates if they receive a worse rating.
In each step, the vote of the new median voter is removed, and this median voter is the one who determined the new majority mention for the candidates.
The candidates have obtained exactly the same distribution of votes.

== Opponent-based tie-breaking rules ==

If the largest value corresponds to opponents of one or more candidates, those candidates lose against other candidates with equal standing.
When candidates A and B have the same majority grade and the largest group of dissatisfied voters corresponds to the opponents of candidate B, candidate B lost against candidate A.
Consequently, candidate A was elected because candidate A was the only remaining candidate.
The largest group of opponents is given the right, and this largest group of opponents then determines the result.

== Recursive calculation of successors ==

The highest calculated value corresponds to the percentage of supporters for multiple candidates, and these candidates defeat other candidates who are still tied.
The highest calculated value corresponds to the percentage of opponents for all remaining tied candidates.
For all candidates still tied, the common group of supporters or opponents is replaced by their successors.
If tied candidates have the same percentage of supporters, the calculation is restarted, and in this restart, the supporters of each candidate are replaced by their successors.
If candidates tied for the majority mention have the same percentage of opponents, the calculation is repeated, and in this case, the opponents of each candidate are replaced by their successors.
The common group is replaced by its successors at each step, and this replacement applies to all candidates involved in the tie.
The replacement process is recursive, and the successors are defined as voters who strictly cast more votes than the common majority mention plus one.
Then the candidates have obtained exactly the same vote distribution.
