# Celurit

A Celurit or Clurit is generally a sickle (sometimes other variants include billhook) with a pronounced crescent-blade patterns which curves more than half a circle and a long handle, is widely used for agricultural purposes and also in Pencak Silat. When compared to the Arit, the Celurit is slightly larger.

== Construction and design ==

The blade of the sickle was originally made of bronze starting from the beginning of our era, after which iron was used, while steel is used in modern times.
The handle is traditionally made of wood and less frequently made of horn.
A bolster is often used to secure the blade in the handle.
The cherurit must have an acute tip.

Although the Celurit (or also generally known as Sabit) is widely used throughout the Indonesian archipelago for agricultural purposes, somehow it is strongly associated with the culture of the Madurese and is frequently used by them as well especially by the leaders who called themselves Sakera.
The sickle has long been the primary tool for Madurese people.
It is possibly used as an agricultural tool in the Banjuwangi region on East Java and then conveyed to Madura.
The sickle is used for harvesting main local crops.
Rice and corn are the main local crops harvested with the sickle.
The sickle is also used for cutting grass.
Besides Arit and Sabit, other variations of the Celurit includes the Arek, Caluk, Calok, Bendo Arit (billhook), Bhiris and so on depending on the geographical area and curvature of the crescent blade.

== Origin and regional variations ==

The sickle known as the 'celurit' is native to Madura.
The celurit has a shape that differs significantly from similar agricultural tools used in most other parts of the Malay Archipelago.
Traditional sickles of many other Indonesian and Malaysian peoples are curved much more strongly and often have rounded tips.

Celurit is also a traditional weapon of the Madurese commonly used in Carok (meaning duel in Madurese language, 'fight in the name of honor'), a style of dueling unlike of those dueling style practiced in their neighbouring island in Sulawesi.
This weapon is also considered as a legendary weapon often associated with the heroic (pre-independence) freedom fighter, Sakera.
The Madurese community are known to attach khodam, a type of mythical creature to abide in the Celurit by a way of prayer before engaging in a carok.
The most famous incident in recent years, a mass carok occurred on 13 July 2006 in Bujur Tengah village, Pamekasan Regency, East Java, Indonesia, resulting stabbing and killing of seven men and seriously injuring nine people.

== Physical characteristics and usage ==

The celurit is worn like a knife or dagger on the belt and is stored in sheaths made from buffalo hide or cowhide.
The shape of the blade facilitates its use as a cold weapon by allowing for chopping strikes and, with some skill, thrusting strikes.

== Historical development and spread ==

Madurese people actively migrated outside their historical area, mainly to north-east Java and also to various regions of Kalimantan, which resulted in the Celurut spreading to other parts of Indonesia.
The combat role of the celurit significantly increased in the second half of the 19th century after Dutch colonizers introduced an official ban on carrying any type of weapon for the Madurese to minimize the possibility of armed rebellion among the Madurese, who were known for their disobedience and warlike spirit.
The sickle became the only weapon available to the islanders, making skills in using the sickle in combat especially in demand and causing techniques using the celurit to become an integral part of a local variant of silat.
The tradition involving this weapon originated in the 18th century.

== Rituals and spiritual beliefs ==

The owner prepares his scythe for battle in advance and reads special spells over the scythe.
These spells are intended to awaken the weapon's combat essence.

== Social significance and traditions ==

The purpose of carok is to protect insulted honor or resolve domestic disputes, and the celurit serves as the only acceptable means of resolving disputes in such conflict situations.
A celurit that won a duel became the owner's source of pride, was shown to family and friends, was often passed down from generation to generation, and was considered a valuable relic.
It was customary not to wipe the bloodstains off the blade to preserve a visible testament of martial prowess for as long as possible.

== Legal status and modern consequences ==

Charok became subject to criminal prosecution in independent Indonesia and was prosecuted as a serious crime.
The practice of openly storing 'victorious' chelurits declined sharply.
There are known cases of lucky participants in charok who sought to preserve the memory of their victory by submitting a false clue to the authorities.
The false clue was a 'chelorit' smeared with animal blood.
These tricks stopped working due to the development of forensic expertise over time.
Maduran duelists were forced to abandon the tradition of commemorating their victories in this way.
The frequent use of chelurita for criminal purposes has caused serious concern among local authorities.

== Manufacturing ==

Blacksmiths specializing in making celurit are traditionally respected in Madura and often perform various religious or mystical rituals related to their work.
This inheritance of skills sometimes results in the formation of well-known working dynasties within their community.
Production centers for chelurits form in various localities, including the village of Petronggan in a Maduran district where most families are engaged in making sickles.
Some families have practiced this craft for centuries.

== Social context ==

Village elders and representatives of the Muslim clergy traditionally enjoy great authority among the Madurese.
