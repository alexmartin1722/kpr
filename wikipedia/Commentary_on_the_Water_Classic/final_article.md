# Commentary on the Water Classic

The Commentary on the Water Classic (), or Commentaries on the Water Classic, commonly known as Shui Jing Zhu, is a work on the Chinese geography in ancient times, describing the traditional understanding of its waterways and ancient canals, compiled by Li Daoyuan during the Northern Wei Dynasty (386-534 AD). The book is divided into sections by river, each described with its source, course, and major tributaries, including cultural and historical notes.

The work is much expanded from its source text, the older (and now lost) Water Classic (Shuijing 水經). The original text described 137 different rivers in China and was traditionally credited to Eastern Han scholar and geographer Sang Qin (桑钦) during the Three Kingdoms Period (220-280 AD). Qing dynasty scholars gave it a later date (during the Three Kingdoms Period) because of the names of the counties and commanderies. Its authorship was then attributed to Jin dynasty scholar Guo Pu. Li Daoyuan's 40-volume, 300,000-character version includes 1252 rivers.

Although very thorough for its time, it did repeat the earlier mistake of the "Tribute of Yu" in viewing the Min river of Sichuan as the headwaters of the Yangtze. It was not until the Ming dynasty that Xu Xiake correctly listed the Jinsha as the principal source.

== Content and scope ==

The work, also known as the 'Classic of Rivers', describes the geography of river catchment areas and provides important information about the land and the people.
Ancient China's waterways are described and illustrated, while one volume describes the Ganges and Indus rivers in India and another describes the hydrology of the Bay of Bengal.
The descriptions include practical anecdotes and myths, which were not included in the earlier text called 'Classic of the Rivers'.
The interpretation includes descriptions of abysses, swamps, islands, bridges, and dams.
This interpretation is the only one that has survived to the present day.
The Commentary is essentially an independent work that uses the Classic of Rivers as a framework.
The 'Commentary' expands on the description of the River Ji, where the 'Classic of Rivers' states that the river originates from the capital of the Three Emperors and empties into the sea to the east in a 16-character passage.
The annotations in the 'Commentary' increase the value of this passage to 6,000 characters.

The interpretation includes descriptions of palaces and tombs, and the distinctions regarding different locations are precise.
Five levels of water temperature for hot springs were determined, the widths of gorges were noted, the depths of channels were measured, and water level changes in different seasons were recorded.
The composition of sand was analyzed, minerals are detailed in the work, and fossils are detailed in the work.
The work includes fossils of human remains, a rich variety of plants is detailed in the work, and a rich variety of animals is detailed in the work.
Inscriptions are depicted in the work, and carvings are depicted in the work.
There are ten compound words for the word 'waterfall', and these compound words are used to distinguish between different types of waterfalls.
The work provides many clarifications in the field of river studies.
There are different versions of the work, and only seven of these versions are considered famous.
It is the most comprehensive geographical work produced in ancient China.

It is the most systematic geographical work produced in ancient China and contains documentation of engravings, artistic works, and many folk songs.
Many of the books cited in the work have been lost over the years, so the work serves as a source for many lost sources.
The author linked the sources of the Yellow River to the Kunlun Mountains based on previous sources, a claim that was refuted several hundred years later by Du You in his book 'Institutes of Movement'.
The author mentions a river that was likely already dry, and it appears that the name is not actually a river name but instead part of a description of a function.
The author could not necessarily visit the documented southern regions himself, so their theories are summarized and contain many errors.

Consequently, the text presents information that is baseless or nonsensical and may even discuss rivers that do not exist.
The text of the book consists of annotations ('Zhu') corresponding to the main text ('Jing').

== Authorship and dating ==

Li Daoyuan lived from 469 to 527 AD and died in 527.
The work is estimated to have been completed in the 4th year of Yanchang (515 AD), an attribution based on the Tang Liu Dian.
Guo Pu (276–324) commented on the work, and the 'Book of the Old Tang' lists him from the Jin Dynasty as the author in its 'Bibliography' section while stating that the 'History of Sui' considers him to be the annotator rather than the author.
The Tongdian examined the representation of place names and stated that the work was established after Emperor Shundi of the Later Han Dynasty.
Yang Shoujing argued that the work was established during the Wei period of the Three Kingdoms era.

== Textual history ==

The work is first mentioned in the book Suoy.
By around the 10th century, some volumes were lost, including three volumes of commentary by Guo Pu from the Jin Dynasty.
The original text and the commentary notes were originally separate entities but became mixed together.
Scholars from the Ming and Qing dynasties investigated and collated the text, resulting in multiple restored versions.
The most detailed textual research was conducted on the 'Shuijing Zhu Jian', which was authored by Zhu Mouyuan of the Ming Dynasty and written in 1615.
Quan Zuo Wang, who lived from 1705 to 1755, and Dai Zhen, who lived from 1723 to 1777, restored the text.
