# Hot-melt adhesive

Hot-melt adhesive (HMA), also known as hot glue, is a form of thermoplastic adhesive that is commonly sold as solid cylindrical sticks of various diameters designed to be applied using a hot glue gun. The gun uses a continuous-duty heating element to melt the plastic glue, which the user pushes through the gun either with a mechanical trigger mechanism on the gun, or with direct finger pressure. The glue squeezed out of the heated nozzle is initially hot enough to burn and even blister skin. The glue is sticky when hot, and solidifies in a few seconds to one minute. Hot-melt adhesives can also be applied by dipping or spraying, and are popular with hobbyists and crafters both for affixing and as an inexpensive alternative to resin casting.

In industrial use, hot-melt adhesives provide several advantages over solvent-based adhesives. Volatile organic compounds are reduced or eliminated, and the drying or curing step is eliminated. Hot-melt adhesives have a long shelf life and usually can be disposed of without special precautions. Some of the disadvantages involve thermal load of the substrate, limiting use to substrates not sensitive to higher temperatures, and loss of bond strength at higher temperatures, up to complete melting of the adhesive. Loss of bond strength can be reduced by using a reactive adhesive that after solidifying undergoes further curing, whether by moisture (e.g., reactive urethanes and silicones), or ultraviolet radiation. Some HMAs may not be resistant to chemical attacks and weathering. HMAs do not lose thickness during solidifying, whereas solvent-based adhesives may lose up to 50–70% of layer thickness during drying.

== History ==

The first systematically manufactured adhesive in human history was a melt adhesive.
Both Neanderthals and modern humans (Homo sapiens of the Cro-Magnon era) used birch tar at least 45,000 years ago to connect stone and wood of their weapons and tools.
The oldest evidence of birch tar usage comes from Campitello, Italy, which is located in the upper Adige valley (Oberes Arnotal), and this evidence is approximately 200,000 years old.
Birch tar was produced from birch bark through a process involving dry distillation. Ötzi was found as an ice mummy in 1991 and died around 3340 BC on the Similaun. Ötzi attached the tips of his flint arrows to shafts made from woolly snowball tree wood using plant fibers and birch tar.
The property of certain materials to melt and solidify upon cooling has been used since antiquity.
Animal glues are used in the wood industry and are applied when hot.
Wax adheres to only one surface.
The growth of this material category followed the rise of plastics.
These products were originally intended for paper coating and were originally made of resins and paraffins.

Sealing wax was the first mass-produced thermoplastic adhesive composition in history.
Hot glue was invented by George Schultz, who was from Boston and 82 years old when he invented hot glue in the late 1940s.
Schultz derived his idea from shoe factory workers who used glue that was heated in a pot.
Hot glue was originally produced in the USA, and the original version of hot glue was manufactured in the United States.
Ethylene-vinyl acetate (EVA) was introduced to the market in the 1960s, and the addition of different polymers allowed for the production of resistant materials.
In the early days, hot melt glue strips were also used by teachers in Taiwan to punish students.

== Terminology and Classification ==

Melting adhesives are also known as hot glues, hotmelt, or Heissleim in Switzerland.
Hot melt adhesive is also known as polyester glue, thermocola, and hot bond.
In Spanish, they are frequently called silicone glue and silicone gun, though this naming is incorrect.
The appearance of cold glue may resemble a silicone sealant, and some packaging and catalogs in Spanish for hot glue guns and sticks often use the word silicone, which contributes further to the spread of both errors.
This classification method is based on the solidification mechanism.
Physically curing adhesives are thermoplastics, while chemically curing adhesives form thermosets.
Thermosets are three-dimensional networks.
Based on the solidification mechanism, melting adhesives are divided into amorphous polymers, semi-crystalline polymers, and high-molecular-weight polymers.
For amorphous polymers, the glass transition temperature is above room temperature.
For semi-crystalline polymers, the glass transition temperature is above room temperature.
For high-molecular-weight polymers, the glass transition temperature is below room temperature.
Plastisols represent a specific category of thermosetting adhesive compositions.
During heating, a chemical hardening reaction occurs in thermosetting adhesives.
Plastisol is a specific category of adhesive compositions.

This category involves thermal curing.
Thermoplastic glue should not be confused with heat-conductive glues.

Melt viscosity One of the most noticeable properties.
Influences the spread of applied adhesive, and the wetting of the surfaces.
Temperature-dependent, higher temperature lowers viscosity.
Melt flow index A value roughly inversely proportional to the molecular weight of the base polymer.
High melt flow index adhesives are easy to apply but have poor mechanical properties due to shorter polymer chains.
Low melt flow index adhesives have better properties but are more difficult to apply.
Pot life stability The degree of stability in molten state, the tendency to decompose and char.
Important for industrial processing where the adhesive is molten for prolonged periods before deposition.
Bond-formation temperature Minimum temperature below which sufficient wetting of substrates does not occur.

== Viscosity and Flow Characteristics ==

The material must have high viscosity when melted and a high Melt Flow Index (MFI) value.
Typical viscosities of melt adhesives at processing temperatures range between 20 Pa·s and 10,000 Pa·s, with polyamide melt adhesives having a typical viscosity of 20 Pa·s and ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymer melt adhesives having a typical viscosity of 10,000 Pa·s at these temperatures.
The melt has a viscosity necessary for optimal wetting at these temperatures.

== Thermal Properties and Processing Temperatures ==

The glass transition temperature of the mixture is below the heat applied by the applicator device.
Processing temperatures typically range between 120 and 240 degrees Celsius.
The processing temperature for polyimide-based adhesives is above 260 °C.
Some adhesives can be processed at temperatures around 100 °C.
The process occurs at approximately 150 to 180 degrees.
The required temperature range for activation is between 80 and 100 degrees Celsius.

== Heat Resistance and Thermal Stability ==

A bond made with a melt adhesive without post-curing has limited heat resistance, with a maximum heat resistance of up to its solidification temperature.
The thermal stability of such a bond is usually below its softening temperature range.

== Mechanical Strength and Elasticity ==

The adhesive joint possesses elasticity.
Hot melt adhesive has hardness characteristics and high strength.
Thermoplastics adhesives and thermoplastics glue have high mechanical strength.
High-temperature glue sticks result in stronger adhesion.

== Gap Filling and Surface Adaptation ==

The adhesive can compensate for unevenness on the bonded surfaces.
Thermoplastics adhesives have the ability to fill gaps, and these properties make thermoplastics adhesives convenient for bonding very uneven surfaces.
Thermoplastics glue can fill gaps, and these properties make thermoplastics glue convenient for bonding very uneven surfaces.

== Physical State and Appearance ==

At room temperature, the stick has an elastic consistency and an opalescent appearance.
These characteristics prevent the formation of thin-layer joints, which are easily achievable with other adhesives.

== Reusability and Cleanability ==

Adhesive residues can be liquefied by heating and removed from most surfaces without residue, and heating residues aids in cleaning tools or removing old bonds.
Thermoplastic adhesive can repeatedly transition to a liquid form (melt) when heated and can transition to a liquid form (melt) multiple times when heated.

== Cost and Safety ==

The adhesive has a low material cost.
Hot melt adhesive is non-toxic.

== Properties and Composition ==

Melting adhesives are solvent-free and water-free.
Thermoplastic glue can be easily removed as solid waste and is recyclable.
Polymerization occurs after the layer melts.
Metal substrates have good thermal conductivity, which causes the adhesive mass to cool down too quickly when bonding to metal.
This rapid cooling of the adhesive mass is caused by the substrate's thermal conductivity and can result in poor adhesion.
Preheating the metal provides a solution.

Open time is the duration between applying the adhesive and assembling the elements.
The opening time ranges from one or two seconds up to several minutes.
Joints in thermally conductive materials must be made quickly.
Joints in thermally insulating materials require more time to cool.
Waiting allows for a strong bond in thermally insulating materials.
Set time is the duration between assembling the elements and the actual bonding.
The glue sets faster if the material being glued can rapidly absorb heat.
The setting and curing time for ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) is tens of seconds.
Tack is the degree of surface stickiness of the adhesive and influences the strength of the bond between wetted surfaces.
Surface energy influences wetting of different kind of surfaces.

Hot-melt glues usually consist of one base material with various additives.
The composition is usually formulated to have a glass transition temperature (onset of brittleness) below the lowest service temperature and a suitably high melt temperature as well.
The degree of crystallization should be as high as possible but within limits of allowed shrinkage.
The melt viscosity and the crystallization rate (and corresponding open time) can be tailored for the application.
Faster crystallization rate usually implies higher bond strength.
To reach the properties of semicrystalline polymers, amorphous polymers would require molecular weights too high and, therefore, unreasonably high melt viscosity; the use of amorphous polymers in hot-melt adhesives is usually only as modifiers.
Some polymers can form hydrogen bonds between their chains, forming pseudo-cross-links which strengthen the polymer.

The natures of the polymer and the additives used to increase tackiness (called tackifiers) influence the nature of mutual molecular interaction and interaction with the substrate.
In one common system, EVA is used as the main polymer, with terpene-phenol resin (TPR) as the tackifier.
The two components display acid-base interactions between the carbonyl groups of vinyl acetate and hydroxyl groups of TPR, complexes are formed between phenolic rings of TPR and hydroxyl groups on the surface of aluminium substrates, and interactions between carbonyl groups and silanol groups on surfaces of glass substrates are formed.
Polar groups, hydroxyls and amine groups can form acid-base and hydrogen bonds with polar groups on substrates like paper or wood or natural fibers.
Nonpolar polyolefin chains interact well with nonpolar substrates.
Good wetting of the substrate is essential for forming a satisfying bond between the adhesive and the substrate.
More polar compositions tend to have better adhesion due to their higher surface energy.
Amorphous adhesives deform easily, tending to dissipate most of mechanical strain within their structure, passing only small loads on the adhesive-substrate interface; even a relatively weak nonpolar-nonpolar surface interaction can form a fairly strong bond prone primarily to a cohesive failure.
The distribution of molecular weights and degree of crystallinity influences the width of melting temperature range.
Polymers with crystalline nature tend to be more rigid and have higher cohesive strength than the corresponding amorphous ones, but also transfer more strain to the adhesive-substrate interface.
Higher molecular weight of the polymer chains provides higher tensile strength and heat resistance.
Presence of unsaturated bonds makes the adhesive more susceptible to autoxidation and UV degradation and necessitates use of antioxidants and stabilizers.

The adhesives are usually clear or translucent, colorless, straw-colored, tan, or amber.
Pigmented versions are also made and even versions with glittery sparkles.
Materials containing polar groups, aromatic systems, and double and triple bonds tend to appear darker than non-polar fully saturated substances; when a water-clear appearance is desired, suitable polymers and additives, e.g. hydrogenated tackifying resins, have to be used.

Increase of bond strength and service temperature can be achieved by formation of cross-links in the polymer after solidification.
This can be achieved by using polymers undergoing curing with residual moisture (e.g., reactive polyurethanes, silicones), exposure to ultraviolet radiation, electron irradiation, or by other methods.

Resistance to water and solvents is critical in some applications.
For example, in textile industry, resistance to dry cleaning solvents may be required.
Permeability to gases and water vapor may or may not be desirable.
Non
-toxicity of both the base materials and additives and absence of odors is important for food packaging.

Mass-consumption disposable products such as diapers necessitate development of biodegradable HMAs.
Research is being performed on e.g., lactic acid polyesters, polycaprolactone with soy protein, etc.

== Base Polymers and Major Classes ==

The most important polymers used for physically curing hot melts are polyamide resins, saturated polyesters, ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymers (EVA), polyolefins, block copolymers (styrene-butadiene-styrene or styrene-isoprene-styrene), and polyimides.
Polyimides are used in high-performance hot-melt adhesives and high-melting melt adhesives are based on polyimides.
Ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymers are used in bulk hot-melt adhesives, polyolefins are used in bulk hot-melt adhesives, and melting adhesives are mostly ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymers.
The actual properties of the adhesive layer regarding adhesion, cohesion, and thermal behavior are determined by the base polymers.
Polyurethane melt adhesives with post-curing are currently the most widely used type.
Thermoplastic adhesives are typically diverse thermoplastic polymeric materials that are most often based on ethylene vinyl acetate or polyamide.
Conventional hot-melt adhesives use basic resins such as polyurethane (PU), polyamide (PA), polyethylene (PE), and ethylene/vinyl acetate (EVA).

== Ethylene-Vinyl Acetate (EVA) Properties ==

Ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) is a low-melting, sticky material that is fluid when melted.
The softening temperature of ethylene vinyl acetate (EVA) is approximately 80°C.
The material is uncolored and has a whitish appearance.
Almost all modern household hot melt adhesives are made of ethylene vinyl acetate.

== Polyamide and Polyolefin Characteristics ==

Polyamides (PA) have a softening temperature of 150°C or higher, which depends on the grade.
Polyamides are harder, stronger, and less fluid when melted than ethylene vinyl acetate.
Some thermoplastic polyolefins have a softening temperature of 150°C or higher and a working temperature between 180°C and 200°C.
Polyolefin glue does not absorb moisture.
High-pressure polyethylene melts at 100–108°C, while low-pressure polyethylene melts at 120–135°C.
Melted polyethylene does not have good adhesion to itself or to other materials and does not always allow materials to be glued together.
Sealing wax is a thermoplastic adhesive composition.

== Thermosetting and Two-Component Systems ==

Thermosets exist in addition to thermoplastics and can be used as hot melts.
Epoxy hot melts are an example of such thermosets.
These adhesives are applied as a dry film beforehand and become adhesive when heated to between 100 and 150 degrees Celsius.
This chemical reaction hardens the material in an irreversible manner and involves polymerization.
An initial strength of the adhesive bond is achieved.
Two-component melt adhesive systems exist.
One component of the system is the hardener, which is surrounded by a polymer layer.
The polymer layer melts upon heating.

== Plasticizers and Tackifying Resins ==

Various additives are required to ensure functionality or to achieve further specific properties.
Long-chain phthalic acid esters, such as dibutyl phthalate and nonyl phthalate, are used as plasticizers for polymers that are not sufficiently flexible.
Tackifying resins can be natural or synthetic, and their addition creates high tack at high temperatures.

== Fillers, Extenders, and Strength Modifiers ==

Chalk and titanium dioxide are used to increase strength and serve as extenders to reduce costs.

== Antioxidants and Stabilizers ==

Antioxidants are classified into primary and secondary types, and light stabilizers are a type of antioxidant.
