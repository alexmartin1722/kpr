# Lifeline video game

Lifeline, released in Japan as , is a 2003 survival horror adventure video game developed and published by Sony Computer Entertainment (and published by Konami in North America) for the PlayStation 2. Set in the near future aboard a space hotel attacked by unidentified monsters, the game follows the player as they direct cocktail waitress Rio Hohenheim to safety while searching for the player's girlfriend Naomi as well as the source of the monster infestation.

Lifeline's defining aspect is its voice user interface: the vast majority of gameplay is conducted by using the PlayStation 2's microphone to issue commands, which are interpreted by the game via speech recognition to control Rio and dictate her movements and actions.

Lifeline was released on January 30, 2003 in Japan and March 2, 2004 in North America; in Japan, it was optionally sold alongside the PlayStation 2 headset. It received generally mixed reviews, with praise for its innovation and potential but criticism for the low reliability of its speech recognition. However, Lifeline still sold well enough to be rereleased in Japan on September 25, 2003 under Sony's The Best budget range, and the game has maintained somewhat of a cult following over the years since its release for its innovative gameplay and the depth of its voice mechanics.

Lifeline is a survival horror adventure game where the player issues orders to Rio Hohenheim as she attempts to escape a monster-infested space station.
The standout feature of Lifeline is its voice user interface in which the player speaks into their microphone to command Rio.
The player never directly controls Rio, nor any other character, at any point in the game; rather, they are required to tell her what to do at any given time, such as directing her where to go, advising her to examine or use objects, or ordering her what to aim for during a battle with a monster.
Such spoken commands include "run", "stop", "dodge", and "turn left", among many others (approximately 500 commands exist), which prompt Rio to perform specific actions and progress throughout the game.
To issue commands, the player must hold the input mic button (the O button on the DualShock controller) before speaking.

The player can access various menus which provide inventory insertions, detailed maps, and commands to unlock multiple parts of the station.
By using the menus available, the player directs Rio in combat, solves puzzles, examines and interfaces with objects of note, and interacts with NPCs.
During a battle, the player can order Rio to maneuver within the battle space, shift focus to certain enemies, or target specific body parts.
Combat perspectives switch between first-person and that of nearby cameras, with the latter more suitable for encounters with numerous foes.
Plot interactions are followed through at the player's general discretion, with Rio inquiring about which path of action to take.

In regular situations, the player can converse with Rio, with the latter sometimes inquiring about it.
The player can play games with her (e.g. challenge each other to tongue twisters, with Rio receiving health if successfully completed), ask her to do sexual things (e.g. to "do a sexy pose", which she may or may not follow through with), or simply engage in small talk with her (e.g. suggest she eat food or shoot at a static object; she may do so, or more commonly explain her rationale for not doing so).
There are also a few Easter egg conversations; for instance, Rio can ask about the player's girlfriend's name, and react with surprise if the player gives her own name or the name of Rio's voice actor.

== Voice Interface and Controls ==

The game is also known as a voice action adventure game.
The setting involves giving voice commands to Rio, a heroine, from behind a monitor in a monitoring room.
Operation with a boom microphone controller is also possible.
Microphone sensitivity varies depending on the microphone model.
The included microphone is generally considered the best option.
Games allowing real-time voice communication with on-screen characters are rare.

== Command Structure and Interaction ==

The detention cell door could be opened remotely from the monitor room.
If the operator is in the guard room and a coffee cup is on the table, they can interact with it by first indicating 'table' as the location to move there and then specifying 'coffee cup' from the items on top to examine it.
There are cases where long phrases must be specified, such as 'the second drawer from the right' or 'the locker on the far left'.
Rio reacts by giving the order 'Please give proper instructions' if the user's reaction is unclear.
If left alone for a while after this, Rio will question whether the user is watching, and if the situation continued to be ignored, the person sat down in seiza (kneeling position) and eventually fell asleep.
The phrase 'What should I do?' can be asked multiple times for checking the current status.
The system responds to unrelated words such as 'Hello' or 'Cute', but usually dismisses these responses with phrases like 'Whatever' or 'Not the time for this'.

== Item Discovery and Examination ==

The protagonist can operate consoles and panels and refer to items, though the protagonist cannot fight effectively and does not know the detailed status of items.
Initially, the names of items were unknown, and only searchable items displayed blinking icons on the screen.
Pressing the square button retrieves information about the item, at which point the item's name and status become known.
Vague terms such as 'green object' or 'square object' can be used to identify the item, and using these vague terms allows the player to determine the item's name.
Some items can trigger derived actions such as drinking, taking, or reading.
Documents are read by the item, and the item rarely eats food.
Players guess forgotten weapon names.

== Character Behavior and Status ==

Rio's communications occasionally experience interference.
The player finds an item called 'chips' during exploration, and finding the chips allows the player to play a mini-game from the title screen.
The ability to play the mini-game depends on the number of chips obtained.
Movement states include walking and slow walking, and these movement states can be instructed.
The command 'Run to the locker' implies starting in a running state, and moving from this state results in reaching the locker while running.
Rio's health points (HP) determine the game status, and if Rio's HP reaches zero, the game ends.
The command 'Heal' consumes a heal capsule.

== Combat Mechanics and Weapons ==

The player initially fights using a handgun and can acquire equipment from rescue forces.
After acquiring the equipment, the player can attack with a machine gun or a launcher.
The launcher has a limited number of rounds, while weapons other than the launcher have no ammunition limit.
Weapons other than the launcher require reloading.
The command 'Fire' allows shooting at a random location or direct shooting when a specific body part is designated.
The command 'Dodge' includes options to dodge right, left, or backward.
There are conversations taking place in an elevator.

== Enemy Types and Weaknesses ==

The enemies include various types such as worm-like, caterpillar-like, squid-like, frog-like, and spider-like creatures.
Each enemy type has unique weaknesses and unique attack methods.
The enemy body parts are displayed at the bottom of the screen, with examples including 'mouth' and 'eye'.
The operator instructs Rio to attack based on these displayed body parts.

== Special Words and Mini-Games ==

Special Words can be found during exploration and used as commands if discovered.
Some Special Words are system-related elements, such as 'Camera Check' and 'Microphone Check'.
Some Special Words are combat moves that can be used in battle, such as 'Low Kick', 'Taunt', and 'Jump'.
Some Special Words appear to be useless mini-games, such as 'Yamanote Line Game' and 'What Is What'.
Winning any of these Special Words restores Rio's health points.

== Scenario Progression and Dialogue ==

The 'Consult' function can be used at any time during exploration.
Users can ask Rio questions from a list called 'Scenario Words'.
The list of scenario words increases as the scenario progresses.
There are items that would typically be found in a medical room.

In 2029, the player character, an unnamed young man only referred to as the "Operator", and his girlfriend Naomi (Sayaka in the Japanese version) attend an opening ceremony for the Japan Space Line's Space Station Hotel, a large space station orbiting Earth serving as a premium hotel for the ultra-rich.
Suddenly, unidentified alien-like monsters attack the festivities, killing most of the guests and staff and separating Naomi and the Operator, who is found unconscious inside a monitor room within the station that was locked from the outside and from which it is impossible to exit.
From the control room, the Operator, having full access to the Space Station Hotel's mechanisms and cameras, comes into contact with Rio Hohenheim, the heroine who has blonde hair, blue eyes, and a short haircut, and who is a cocktail waitress and subordinate to the station manager Sagitani; she survived the initial attack after being locked in a holding cell for her own safety, left the detention cell, and is attempting to contact the control room.
Communicating with Rio through her headset, the Operator agrees to help her after she asks for assistance searching for something in the hotel and proposes to find Sayaka in exchange for his cooperation, a deal the protagonist accepts, while also attempting to find Naomi and investigate the source of the monsters.
After fighting their way through monsters, clearing difficult puzzles, and coming across both fellow survivors and a paramilitary rescue force, Rio and the Operator learn that the monsters are not extraterrestrials, but rather horribly-mutated humans resulting from an attempt to recreate the philosopher's stone that, instead of healing someone, cursed them to remain alive and drastically mutated them beyond recognition.
They also learn that the stone needed to be created in a zero-gravity environment and that the station is now on a collision course with Earth to return the stone for use as a weapon.
Along the way, Rio meets with her father, now a disillusioned, disembodied brain in a jar incapable of recognizing her, while the Operator reunites with Naomi, who has since been mutated but is still holding on to her humanity; both die after meeting the pair.
The station's experimental building is led by Joe Powers, the head of research voiced by Akira Arimoto, who employs assistant researcher Allen Ozawa, voiced by Shinichiro Miki, who continues to pretend to be dead even after talking with Rio.
Prime Minister Takayama, voiced by Iizuka Etsushi, was invited to the hotel's completion party.
In the end, Rio and the Operator destroy the lead monster, and they meet in person for the first time.
Now adamant to destroy the stone, Rio and the Operator set the Space Station Hotel to self-destruct and manage to reach an escape pod in time.
Rio thanks the Operator as the escape pod starts a descent towards Earth.

== Characters ==

Captain Yatsumeda is voiced by Masao Okada and leads the rescue team.
Muto is a security guard whose actions are overly rigid and honest and mostly fail to achieve their intended results.
Muto secretly harbors feelings for Rio and, concerned about Rio's safety, confines her to a detention cell.
Reiako Ando is a prominent news anchor for a certain television station who is voiced by Eiko Aizawa, and the reporter's purpose for being there was to conduct news coverage.
Sayaka is voiced by Hikari Tachibana.

== Rescue team ==

The Prime Minister mentioned that the rescue team might be from the 1st Airborne Regiment at Narashino or from the Metropolitan Police Department's Special Assault Team (SAT), though the details regarding the identity of the rescue team are unclear.
The rescue team was heavily equipped.

Voice input type games were released before this game was launched.
Examples of such games included 'Seaman' and 'Pikachu Genki de Chu'.
It was considered difficult to adopt voice input systems in action games.
The game included a system to verify player voice suitability for the game.
This verification was achieved by having players practice during the tutorial before the game started.

Lifeline received "mixed" reviews according to video game review aggregator Metacritic.
The game received high praise for its scenario and its characters.
The voice command system, though the game's primary feature, has often been noted to be its game's weak point, due to inaccurate actions taken when commands are given, and the basic sense of conversation and directions reduced to simple verbs and nouns, particularly when in the course of solving many of the game's puzzles.
Players often felt stressed because their commands were not recognized in time during critical situations such as battles against enemies.
IGN noted that while the voice recognition system is "quite deep", the player "will need to practice enunciating regular words and learning the speed at which the game best responds", and "might spend five minutes trying to get the right word to simply inspect a worthless book."
In 2008, Game Informer listed Lifeline among the worst horror games of all time.
In 2009, GamesRadar included it among the games "with untapped franchise potential", commenting: "While this frustrated many PS2 owners, it made others feel more attached to the characters.
Improvements in headset and voice-recognition technology make a franchise more viable on today's systems."
The game received a score of 37 in Famitsu's Cross Review.
A score of 37 in Famitsu's Cross Review qualifies the game for Platinum Hall of Fame status.
