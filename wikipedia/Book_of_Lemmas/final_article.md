# Book of Lemmas

The Book of Lemmas or Book of Assumptions (Arabic Maʾkhūdhāt Mansūba ilā Arshimīdis) is a book attributed to Archimedes by Thābit ibn Qurra, though the authorship of the book is questionable. It consists of fifteen propositions (lemmas) on circles.

== Alternative titles ==

The Book of Lemmas, a mathematics book, is also known as the Book of Auxiliary Propositions.

A commentary on the text was preserved from the 10th century and was written by Alī ibn Ahmad al-Nasawī.
The work Liber Assumptorum is not preserved in Greek.
The publication appeared in Giovanni A.
Borelli's work Apollonii Pergaei Conicorum lib. in volumes V, VI, and VII.
Thomas L.
Heath included this version in his collection The Works of Archimedes in 1897.
A translation by Klem is provided next to Heath's text.
Fifteen of the propositions are included in Clemens' German translation.
All sections are illustrated in the Latin version.
The English version illustrates all sections except for sentence 7.
The German version illustrates all sections except for sentence 7.

The Book of Lemmas was first introduced in Arabic by the Arab geometer Thābit ibn Qurra, who translated the manuscript from Greek into Arabic in the 9th century and attributed the work to Archimedes.
The title of the translation is K. al-Ma'hūdāt.
A translation from Arabic into Latin by John Greaves and revised by Samuel Foster (c.
1650) was published in 1659 as Lemmata Archimedis.
Another Latin translation by Abraham Ecchellensis and edited by Giovanni A.
Borelli was published in 1661 under the name Liber Assumptorum, which translates to 'Mathematical Garland'.
T.
L.
Heath translated Heiburg's Latin work into English in his The Works of Archimedes.
A more recently discovered manuscript copy of Thābit ibn Qurra's Arabic translation was translated into English by Emre Coşkun in 2018 and titled Book of Lemmas.

The original authorship of the Book of Lemmas has been in question because in proposition four, the book refers to Archimedes in third person; however, it has been suggested that it may have been added by the translator.
Heath discusses the question of Archimedes' authorship.
Another possibility is that the Book of Lemmas may be a collection of propositions by Archimedes later collected by a Greek writer.

The Book of Lemmas introduces several new geometrical figures.

Archimedes first introduced the arbelos (shoemaker's knife) in proposition four of his book.
The text quotes the phrase 'quam vocat Archimedes ARBELON'.
The Arbelos (the 'cobbler's knife' mentioned above) can be attributed to Archimedes with high probability.
The figure is used in propositions four through eight.
In propositions five, Archimedes introduces the Archimedes's twin circles, and in proposition eight, he makes use what would be the Pappus chain, formally introduced by Pappus of Alexandria.
The incircle of the Arbelos is introduced in Proposition 6.
The incircle of the Arbelos is related to the Pappus Chain.
Kliem notes this relationship in a footnote.

Archimedes first introduced the salinon (salt cellar) in proposition fourteen of his book.
The Salinon (the 'salt barrel') can be attributed to Archimedes with high probability.
Archimedes proved that the salinon and the circle are equal in area.

== General Structure and Themes ==

Each statement is individually referred to as a 'Propositio'.
The sections contain statements about secants and tangents.
The statements refer to topics in stereometry.
The text provides a complete overview of the central themes of 15 auxiliary sentences.
These two propositions were previously formulated by him as Proposition 33 and Proposition 34.

== Proposition-Specific Topics ==

Sentence 2 covers semicircles, diameters, tangents, and secants.
Sentence 5 covers the arbelos, Archimedes' twin circles, semicircles, and circles.
Sentence 6 covers the arbelos, semicircles, and circles.
Sentence 8 covers trisection of an angle and neusis construction.
Sentence 10 covers tangents to a circle, secants and chords, and parallels.
Sentence 11 covers perpendicular chords and radius.
Sentence 12 covers semicircles, diameters, chords, tangents, and perpendiculars.
Sentence 13 covers circles, diameters, chords, and perpendiculars.
Sentence 14 covers the salinon, semicircles, perpendiculars, circles, and areas.
Sentence 15 covers circles, diameters, inscribed regular pentagons, arcs, centers, perpendiculars, and radii.

== Angle Trisection and Construction Methods ==

The method of trisecting an angle presented in Proposition 8 can be attributed to Archimedes with high probability and divides an angle into three equal parts.
This method uses a straightedge, a compass, and neusis construction.
Claim 8 in the book contains Archimedes' method.
In claim 6, he uses what is later called the Pappus chain.

== Geometric proofs and constructions ==

Point H lies on the extension of line segment BT beyond point T.
According to Thales' theorem, angle ADB is a right angle.
Angle BDH is a right angle.
Point D lies on the Thales circle constructed over segment BH.
Point T is the center of the Thales circle constructed over segment BH.
The length of segment BT is equal to the length of segment TD.
The length of segment BT is equal to the length of segment TH.
Point F is the midpoint of segment DE.
Line segments DE and BH are parallel.
Triangle AED is similar to triangle ABH.
Point T is the midpoint of segment BH.
Point F is the midpoint of segment DE.
Trisecting an arbitrary angle requires a Neusis construction.
A Neusis construction is a specific geometric method.
Archimedes left the procedure of the so-called insertion (Neusis) open.
The insertion is demonstrated using a marked ruler.
A complete proof of these fifteen propositions can be found in a publication by the Royal Spanish Mathematical Society.
The publication is titled 'El Libro de los Lemas'.

== Relation to On the Sphere and Cylinder ==

Archimedes treated a theorem of stereometry in the work 'On Sphere and Cylinder' [cite: 17].
He was the first to determine the exact relationship between the volume and surface area of a sphere and a circular cylinder using methods that are considered precursors to modern integral calculus [cite: 18,19].
The base area of the cylinder corresponds to the largest great circle of the sphere, and the height of the cylinder corresponds to the diameter of the sphere.
The ratio of the surface areas of the two bodies is 3:2, and the ratio of the volumes of the two bodies is 3:2.
Archimedes presented the aforementioned theorem in Book I of On the Sphere and Cylinder as a corollary to two propositions [cite: 20,21].
The surface area of a sphere is four times the area of its greatest circle.
For a sphere, the volume is four times the volume of a cone whose base corresponds to the largest circle of the sphere and whose height corresponds to the radius of the sphere.
The following statement is sometimes referred to as Archimedes' theorem [cite: 22].
Archimedes' theorem states that the volume of a hemisphere equals the difference between the volumes of the surrounding cylinder and the inscribed cone [cite: 22].
The volume of a hemisphere is equal to the difference between the volumes of the surrounding circular cylinder and the inscribed circular cone.
The cylinder and the cone have the same height and the same base area.
